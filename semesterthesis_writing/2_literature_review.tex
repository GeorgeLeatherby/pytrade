\section{Literature Review}

The PyTrade architecture separates two functions that are often learned jointly: extracting a temporally informed signal for one asset and allocating capital across a set of assets. This review develops the rationale for that separation in four steps. First, it frames financial portfolio management as a continuous sequential-control problem. Second, it examines why temporal memory may be useful under partial observability and how feature engineering can reduce the non-stationarity of price-level inputs while retaining relevant historical information. Third, it considers self-attention as a mechanism for cross-sectional comparison. Finally, it locates the resulting SAA--PAA division within the reinforcement learning literature.

\subsection{Sequential Decision-Making and Continuous Control in Finance}

Tactical asset-allocation and time-series-momentum research supply the financial motivation for reacting to information in a price history rather than holding a static allocation indefinitely. Faber \cite{Faber2007} studies a quantitative tactical-allocation rule based on long-term trend signals. His model uses a trend-following system based on a 10-month simple moving average (SMA), where the buy rule is to purchase when the monthly price is above the 10-month SMA, and the sell rule is to sell and move to cash (90-day Treasury bills) when the monthly price is below the 10-month SMA. Moskowitz, Ooi, and Pedersen \cite{Moskowitz2012} document time-series momentum (TSM) across multiple asset classes. TSM is an asset pricing anomaly characterized by strong positive predictability from a security's own past returns, specifically finding that the past 12-month excess return of each instrument is a positive predictor of its future return. While neither contribution applies a reinforcement-learning method, they observe that an instrument's own past return can contain decision-relevant information. Turning that observation into daily, cost-aware rebalancing decisions requires a framework that represents the effect of a decision on the next portfolio state.

Reinforcement learning provides that framework through a Markov Decision Process (MDP). At each decision date, an agent observes a \emph{state} $s_t$, selects an \emph{action} $a_t$, receives a scalar \emph{reward} $r_t$, and transitions to a subsequent state according to the environment's dynamics \cite{SuttonBarto}. In portfolio management, the state includes market and portfolio information, the action is a trade or target allocation, and the reward measures the result after price movements and trading costs \cite{LopezdePrado.2018}. The essential distinction from a one-step forecasting problem is that an action changes cash, holdings, future feasible trades, and transaction costs even when historical prices themselves are exogenous \cite{Sutiene2024}. An MDP only specifies the decision problem; it does not prescribe how to map a high-dimensional state to an action. Deep neural networks supply capable function approximators for this mapping \cite{Goodfellow-et-al-2016}. López de Prado \cite{LopezdePrado.2018} supports the successful use of deep neural networks as function approximators in financial decision making, however his triple-barrier method is a supervised-learning labeling procedure that classifies a realized price path by profit-taking, stop-loss, and time barriers. It is useful for defining discrete labels, but it does not itself determine continuously sized, multi-asset allocations. In a policy-gradient method, the policy network directly parameterizes a probability distribution over actions and adjusts its parameters to increase expected cumulative reward \cite{SuttonBarto}. This makes continuous control natural: the policy can output a real-valued trade or a vector of target portfolio weights rather than selecting from a fixed menu of decisions. 

Policy-gradient updates can be unstable when a new policy moves too far from the policy that generated the training data. Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) addresses this by maximizing a clipped surrogate objective: probability-ratio changes that would improve the objective beyond a chosen interval are clipped, limiting the incentive for an excessively large update \cite{JohnSchulmanFilipWolskiPrafullaDhariwalAlecRadfordOlegKlimov.2017}. PPO also permits several minibatch epochs over a sampled batch of trajectories, giving a practical balance between sample use and implementation simplicity. This is the optimization method used for both PyTrade layers. Junfeng et al. \cite{Junfeng.2024} place reinforcement learning in a portfolio-management setting and investigate several feature extractor networks such as convolutional, long short-term memory (LSTM) and convolutional LSTM networks. They also experiment with trading frequencies and identify the highest risk-adjusted performance in their setting when allowing the agent to trade once per month. The FinRL framework of Liu et al. \cite{liu2022finrldeepreinforcementlearning} operationalizes the portfolio allocation problem through simulated trading environments, common DRL algorithms, transaction costs, risk preferences, backtesting, and reproducible baselines. Implementing PyTrade's two-stage frozen-signal interface within FinRL would require project-specific extensions beyond its standard portfolio-allocation environments. These studies motivate treating the simulator, reward, and evaluation protocol as decisive parts of the learning system rather than as incidental infrastructure.

\subsection{Sequential Representation and Temporal Memory in Non-Stationary Markets}

The MDP formulation becomes only an approximation when an agent sees a limited market summary rather than the full process that generates returns. Market-relevant information, including macroeconomic conditions, investor behavior, and unexpected events, is only partially captured by commonly available daily market data \cite{Zhang2020}. This motivates a Partially Observable MDP (POMDP) definition, in which an observation $o_t$ is an incomplete projection of the underlying state $s_t$. The practical question arising is: how can a policy retain useful evidence from earlier observations without assuming that a single day's feature vector is sufficient?

Hausknecht and Stone \cite{hausknecht2015deep} show this general strategy in Deep Recurrent Q-Learning: replacing a feed-forward layer with an LSTM lets the network integrate a sequence of partial observations and can make it more robust when observability changes. They use Atari games and test the effectiveness of recurrence by reducing the number of available frames the agent receives as input. The LSTM, originally proposed by Hochreiter and Schmidhuber \cite{Hochreiter1997}, maintains a cell state controlled by input, forget, and output gates. The gates regulate what is written, retained, and exposed, allowing gradients and relevant information to persist over longer intervals than in a simple recurrent network. PyTrade uses this principle in the SAA to test whether an isolated asset's current technical features acquire meaning from their recent path and the agent's own previous positions.

Introducing network memory alone would not be sufficient for processing the available raw prices (open, high, low \& close) and trading-volume information (OHLCV) directly as an observation by a DRL agent. Financial price levels are typically non-stationary, as many major stock market indices reach new records in most decades \cite{Schmitt_2013, Cabral2026}. A feature whose scale drifts between training and evaluation makes the learned mapping fragile and can introduce domain shift \cite{LopezdePrado.2018}. Integer differencing and log returns reduce this problem but discard much of the level information that can express a slow-moving trend. López de Prado \cite{LopezdePrado.2018} proposes fractional differentiation as a controlled compromise: a non-integer difference order can make a series more stationary while retaining a decaying memory of historical price level. This trade-off directly informs PyTrade's fractionally differentiated, normalized price feature and its separate rolling return, volatility, volume, and cross-asset features described in Section~\ref{sec:state}.

Recent forecasting literature demonstrates that temporal representation need not be exclusively recurrent. Temporal Fusion Transformers combine local recurrent processing, gating and self-attention to select features and capture longer temporal dependencies for interpretable multi-horizon forecasts \cite{lim2020tft}. Shen et al. \cite{LefeiShen.2025} further compare Transformer design choices for long-term time-series forecasting, finding that architectural decisions such as attention direction and forecast aggregation materially affect results. These are forecasting results, however, not evidence that a particular forecasting architecture is optimal for an online trading policy. PyTrade therefore uses a recurrent SAA for compact, stepwise state updating, while reserving attention for the allocation problem rather than claiming that LSTMs are universally preferable to Transformers. The next section defines self-attention and explains its role as a cross-sectional comparison mechanism.

\subsection{Self-Attention and Cross-Sectional Asset Interaction}
\label{sec:self-attention}

The temporal SAA answers only part of the portfolio problem, as it finds isolated trading signals for individual assets. A positive signal for one asset need not justify a large allocation when another asset offers a stronger opportunity, when the candidates move together, or when current portfolio exposure already concentrates the same risk. These are cross-sectional comparisons: they concern relations among assets at one decision date rather than the chronological evolution of one asset. Term structures, volatility skews, and changing correlations across asset classes make this distinction especially relevant for risk management \cite{Bennett.2014}.

Self-attention enables each token in a sequence to construct a representation based on a weighted combination of the other tokens, with the weights reflecting their relevance to that token \cite{vaswani2017}. Transformers are architectures that use stacked self-attention and feed-forward layers, together with positional information and normalization mechanisms, to process sequences while modeling relationships between their elements \cite{manning2023_stanford}. Given appropriate token representations, they project each token into a query, key, and value. The compatibility between a query and all keys is normalized into weights and used to aggregate the corresponding values. Multi-head attention repeats this computation in several learned representation subspaces, allowing different relation types to be represented simultaneously \cite{vaswani2017}. With shared token-wise transformations, permutation-preserving attention operations, and no asset-order or asset-identity encoding, an attention block applied to a set of asset tokens is permutation equivariant: permuting the input tokens permutes the corresponding outputs. This is appropriate for PyTrade's PAA, whose asset tokens contain per-asset market features and transferred SAA signals, while a portfolio token carries shared portfolio information.

The original Transformer demonstrated that self-attention can connect all input positions directly and parallelize their processing, but it did so for sequence transduction, not portfolio optimization \cite{vaswani2017}. Later work maps attention into reinforcement-learning settings. Hu et al. \cite{Hu.2024} review how Transformers have been used to model trajectories and multimodal decision context, while Kachaev et al. \cite{NikitaKachaev.2025} examine policy/value-network design, input conditioning, and sequence slicing for online, model-free continuous control. Their results make Transformers credible continuous-control baselines, but do not remove the need to test the chosen attention representation and reward design in a financial environment.

This limitation matters because empirical covariance matrices summarize linear co-movement over a chosen historical window. An attention allocator can condition a pairwise comparison on the observed regime, but it is not guaranteed to discover useful diversification or to remain stable during a regime shift. Xue and Ye \cite{Xue.2026} propose a framework which integrates per-asset temporal encoders with a global attention layer and use a flat Dirichlet policy to parameterize the distribution of portfolio weights directly on the simplex, enforcing budget and non-negativity constraints by construction. They report improved financial performance compared to a passive buy and hold SPDR S\&P 500 ETF Trust (SPY). Coulter \cite{Coulter2025} evaluates an attention-based Transformer architecture for direct portfolio weight allocation against classical and LSTM portfolio allocators across a diverse set of asset classes and market regimes. The study reports superior risk-adjusted performance and a higher cumulative return than the LSTM baseline over its evaluation period. \subsection{Modular Decomposition and Hierarchical Policy Architectures}
\label{sec:modular-composition}

The preceding strands motivate a division of labor: recurrent memory may transform each asset's history into a compact temporal representation \cite{Junfeng.2024}, while attention may compare those representations across the asset universe \cite{Coulter2025}. A monolithic policy can in principle learn both transformations from portfolio reward alone, but it must simultaneously discover temporal features, cross-asset interactions, and constraints on executable allocations. With limited and non-stationary financial data, this creates a difficult credit-assignment problem: a poor allocation can arise from a weak single-asset signal, an incorrect cross-sectional comparison, or the interaction between both.

Related financial work has explored hybrid LSTM--PPO portfolio architectures. Kevin and Yugopuspito \cite{JunKevin.2025} present a hybrid architecture combining LSTM networks for return forecasting with a PPO reinforcement learning agent for dynamic, transaction-aware portfolio allocation, evaluating its performance across multi-asset data. They report that the hybrid LSTM plus PPO portfolio achieved the highest annualized return of 0.2538 (25.4\%) against all benchmarks and single-model baselines during the 2024 test period, though it also exhibited relatively high volatility. Raj \cite{GabrielNixonRaj.2025} compares PPO, LSTM-PPO, and Transformer-PPO under simulated regime changes, volatility penalties, and tail-risk shocks using a custom Gym environment. He reports that Transformer-PPO obtained the strongest risk-adjusted results in its setting, whereas LSTM variants offered a lower computational cost, more interpretable trade-off. These studies support testing architectural specialization under changing conditions, but they do not establish that a signal learned on one asset should be transferred unchanged to a separate allocator. 

Another approach to modularity is presented by  Charkhestani and Esfahanipour \cite{Charkestani.2026}. They propose a behaviorally informed deep reinforcement learning agent for algorithmic portfolio optimization, which integrates two well-established behavioral biases, loss aversion and overconfidence, into an actor–critic architecture. Unlike conventional DRL systems that assume fully rational agents, the proposed framework incorporates investor heterogeneity through regime-dependent bias thresholds that adjust position sizing, while the underlying RL policy determines trading direction.

The Charkhestani and Esfahanipour framework represents modularity in model assumptions rather than policy composition. PyTrade does not model any behavioral factors explicitly, but investigates the interaction between hierarchical elements. Consistent with the task-decomposition perspective in hierarchical reinforcement learning, PyTrade uses an architectural hierarchy in which a lower-level single-asset policy supplies signals to a portfolio-level policy; it does not implement temporal options or subgoal-based control \cite{Pateria2021}. The SAA is trained first on a single-asset task, frozen, and then used to generate per-asset signals that form part of the PAA's observation. The PAA remains the sole policy that executes the multi-asset allocation. Freezing the SAA isolates the transfer question because a PAA outcome cannot be attributed to both a changing source signal and a changing allocator at the same time. Section~\ref{sec:system-design} details the resulting POMDPs, feature pipeline, reward design, simulator, and out-of-sample evaluation.

The literature thus supports the individual choices of PPO, recurrent temporal state, and attention-based cross-sectional processing, but provides limited evidence on their controlled combination through a frozen signal-transfer interface. The contribution of this thesis is not to presume that the combination must dominate a monolithic model. Instead, it measures how the interface affects risk-adjusted performance, learning stability, turnover, drawdowns, and allocation behavior against matched ablations. That comparison makes it possible to distinguish a genuinely useful temporal signal from one that is redundant, harmful, or only conditionally useful in particular market regimes.
