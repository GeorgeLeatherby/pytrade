\section{Literature Review}

The PyTrade architecture separates two functions that are often learned jointly: extracting a temporally informed signal for one asset and allocating capital across a set of assets. This review develops the rationale for that separation in four steps. First, it frames financial portfolio management as a continuous sequential-control problem. Second, it examines why temporal memory may be useful under partial observability and how feature engineering can reduce the non-stationarity of price-level inputs while retaining relevant historical information. Third, it considers self-attention as a mechanism for cross-sectional comparison. Finally, it locates the resulting SAA--PAA division within the reinforcement learning literature.

\subsection{Sequential Decision-Making and Continuous Control in Finance}

Tactical asset-allocation and time-series-momentum research supply the financial motivation for reacting to information in a price history rather than holding a static allocation indefinitely. Faber \cite{Faber2007} studies a quantitative tactical-allocation rule based on long-term trend signals. His model uses a trend-following system based on a 10-month simple moving average (SMA), where the buy rule is to purchase when the monthly price is above the 10-month SMA, and the sell rule is to sell and move to cash (90-day Treasury bills) when the monthly price is below the 10-month SMA. Moskowitz, Ooi, and Pedersen \cite{Moskowitz2012} document time-series momentum (TSM) across multiple asset classes. TSM is an asset pricing anomaly characterized by strong positive predictability from a security's own past returns, specifically finding that the past 12-month excess return of each instrument is a positive predictor of its future return. While neither contribution applies a reinforcement-learning method, they observe that an instrument's own past return can contain decision-relevant information. Turning that observation into daily, cost-aware rebalancing decisions requires a framework that represents the effect of a decision on the next portfolio state.

Reinforcement learning represents this problem through a Markov Decision Process (MDP). The Markov assumption means that the current state contains the information needed to describe the next-state distribution, conditional on the action. At each decision date, an agent observes a \emph{state} $s_t$, selects an \emph{action} $a_t$, receives a scalar \emph{reward} $\mathcal R_{t+1}$, and transitions according to the environment's dynamics \cite{SuttonBarto}. In portfolio management, the state contains market and account information, the action is a trade or target allocation, and the reward measures performance after price movements and costs \cite{LopezdePrado.2018}. Unlike one-step forecasting, a decision changes cash, holdings, and future feasible trades even when historical prices are exogenous, meaning unaffected by the agent \cite{Sutiene2024}. In this setting neural networks approximate the mapping from observations to decisions \cite{Goodfellow-et-al-2016}. During the training a policy-gradient method adjusts an action distribution to increase expected cumulative reward \cite{SuttonBarto}; continuous control therefore permits real-valued trades or allocation weights. This differs from supervised labeling, such as the triple-barrier method, which assigns price paths to profit-taking, stop-loss, or time-limit outcomes rather than directly optimizing allocation decisions \cite{LopezdePrado.2018}.

Policy-gradient updates can be unstable when a new policy differs substantially from the policy that generated its training data. PPO limits the incentive for large updates by clipping improvements associated with action-probability ratios outside a chosen interval \cite{JohnSchulmanFilipWolskiPrafullaDhariwalAlecRadfordOlegKlimov.2017}. It can reuse sampled trajectories (sequences of observations, actions, and rewards) over several optimization passes. Both PyTrade layers use this method. Junfeng et al. \cite{Junfeng.2024} compare convolutional networks, which detect local patterns, and long short-term memory (LSTM) networks, which retain a learned summary of earlier observations. They find the highest risk-adjusted performance in their setting when trading monthly. FinRL, the financial reinforcement-learning framework of Liu et al. \cite{liu2022finrldeepreinforcementlearning}, combines simulated trading, DRL algorithms, transaction costs, backtesting, and reproducible baselines. PyTrade's frozen-signal interface requires additional support for isolated SAA accounts. These studies motivate treating the simulator, reward, and evaluation protocol as parts of the learning system, not incidental infrastructure.

\subsection{Sequential Representation and Temporal Memory in Non-Stationary Markets}

The MDP formulation becomes an approximation when the agent sees only a market summary. Macroeconomic conditions, investor behavior, and unexpected events are only partly captured by daily data \cite{Zhang2020}. A Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP) therefore distinguishes the underlying state $s_t$ from an incomplete observation $o_t$. The practical question is how a policy can retain useful evidence when one day's feature vector is insufficient.

Hausknecht and Stone \cite{hausknecht2015deep} demonstrate this strategy in deep recurrent Q-learning, which estimates the expected discounted reward of actions while retaining observation history. Replacing a feed-forward layer with an LSTM helps integrate partial observations; their Atari experiments reduce the number of visible frames to test this effect. The LSTM, proposed by Hochreiter and Schmidhuber \cite{Hochreiter1997}, maintains a cell state controlled by input, forget, and output gates. These regulate what is written, retained, and exposed. PyTrade uses this principle to test whether current single-asset features gain meaning from their recent path and the agent's previous positions.

Network memory alone does not solve the input-scaling problem. Open, high, low, and close prices and trading volume (OHLCV) describe each trading session, but price-level statistics can change over time \cite{Schmitt_2013,Cabral2026}. Changes in the input distribution between training and evaluation, termed \emph{domain shift}, can weaken a learned decision rule \cite{LopezdePrado.2018}. Integer differencing and log returns remove price-level information, including some slow-moving trend context. Fractional differentiation offers a compromise: a non-integer difference order can reduce non-stationarity while retaining a decaying contribution from earlier prices \cite{LopezdePrado.2018}. This motivates the normalized price, return, volatility, and volume features in Section~\ref{sec:state}.

Recent forecasting literature demonstrates that temporal representation need not be exclusively recurrent. Temporal Fusion Transformers combine local recurrent processing, gating and self-attention to select features and capture longer temporal dependencies for interpretable multi-horizon forecasts \cite{lim2020tft}. Shen et al. \cite{LefeiShen.2025} further compare Transformer design choices for long-term time-series forecasting, finding that architectural decisions such as attention direction and forecast aggregation materially affect results. These are forecasting results, however, not evidence that a particular forecasting architecture is optimal for an online trading policy. PyTrade therefore uses a recurrent SAA for compact, stepwise state updating, while reserving attention for the allocation problem rather than claiming that LSTMs are universally preferable to Transformers. The next section defines self-attention and explains its role as a cross-sectional comparison mechanism.

\subsection{Self-Attention and Cross-Sectional Asset Interaction}
\label{sec:self-attention}

The SAA answers only part of the portfolio problem. A positive signal need not justify a large allocation when another asset offers a stronger opportunity or current holdings concentrate similar risks. These are cross-sectional comparisons, concerning relations among assets at one decision date. Changing correlations, term structures (how yields or volatility vary with maturity), and volatility skews (how option-implied volatility varies across strike prices) make these relations relevant for risk management \cite{Bennett.2014}.

Self-attention constructs each \emph{token}, a feature vector representing one entity, from a weighted combination of the other tokens \cite{vaswani2017}. Transformers combine attention with feed-forward transformations and normalization \cite{manning2023_stanford}. Each token supplies a \emph{query} describing what it seeks, a \emph{key} used to assess its relevance, and a \emph{value} containing information to aggregate. Query--key compatibility determines the aggregation weights. Multi-head attention repeats this operation in several learned subspaces, allowing different relationships to be represented simultaneously \cite{vaswani2017}. Without order-specific information, shared attention is permutation equivariant: reordering tokens reorders their outputs. This motivates shared cross-asset processing, although PyTrade also supplies asset identities and uses a fixed ordering for its final allocation outputs.

The original Transformer demonstrated that self-attention can connect all input positions directly and parallelize their processing, but it did so for sequence transduction, not portfolio optimization \cite{vaswani2017}. Later work maps attention into reinforcement-learning settings. Hu et al. \cite{Hu.2024} review how Transformers have been used to model trajectories and multimodal decision context, while Kachaev et al. \cite{NikitaKachaev.2025} examine policy/value-network design, input conditioning, and sequence slicing for online, model-free continuous control. Their results make Transformers credible continuous-control baselines, but do not remove the need to test the chosen attention representation and reward design in a financial environment.

Empirical covariance matrices summarize linear co-movement over a historical window. Attention can condition comparisons on the current regime, but does not guarantee useful diversification or stability. Xue and Ye \cite{Xue.2026} combine per-asset temporal encoders with global attention and a Dirichlet policy, a probability distribution over non-negative weights that sum to one. This constrains allocations to the portfolio simplex, the set of fully allocated long-only weight vectors. They report improvements over SPY buy-and-hold. Coulter \cite{Coulter2025} compares attention-based allocation with classical and LSTM allocators across asset classes and regimes, reporting stronger risk-adjusted performance and cumulative return than the LSTM baseline in that evaluation. Section~\ref{sec:modular-composition} considers how these components can be combined.

\subsection{Modular Decomposition and Hierarchical Policy Architectures}
\label{sec:modular-composition}

The preceding strands motivate a division of labor: recurrent memory may transform each asset's history into a compact temporal representation \cite{Junfeng.2024}, while attention may compare those representations across the asset universe \cite{Coulter2025}. A monolithic policy can in principle learn both transformations from portfolio reward alone, but it must simultaneously discover temporal features, cross-asset interactions, and constraints on executable allocations. With limited and non-stationary financial data, this creates a difficult credit-assignment problem: a poor allocation can arise from a weak single-asset signal, an incorrect cross-sectional comparison, or the interaction between both.

Related financial work explores hybrid LSTM--PPO portfolio architectures. Kevin and Yugopuspito \cite{JunKevin.2025} combine recurrent return forecasting with cost-aware portfolio allocation, reporting an annualized return of 25.4\% in their 2024 test period, alongside relatively high volatility. Raj \cite{GabrielNixonRaj.2025} compares recurrent and attention-based policies under simulated regime changes, volatility penalties, and tail-risk shocks (unusually large adverse outcomes). Attention-based policies provide the strongest risk-adjusted results in that setting, while recurrent alternatives use less computation. These findings motivate specialization, but do not establish the value of transferring frozen SAA signals to a separate PAA.

Another approach to modularity is presented by  Charkhestani and Esfahanipour \cite{Charkestani.2026}. They propose a behaviorally informed deep reinforcement learning agent for algorithmic portfolio optimization, which integrates two well-established behavioral biases, loss aversion and overconfidence, into an actor–critic architecture. Unlike conventional DRL systems that assume fully rational agents, the proposed framework incorporates investor heterogeneity through regime-dependent bias thresholds that adjust position sizing, while the underlying RL policy determines trading direction.

The Charkhestani and Esfahanipour framework represents modularity in model assumptions rather than policy composition. PyTrade does not model any behavioral factors explicitly, but investigates the interaction between the SAA and hierarchical PAA. Consistent with the task-decomposition perspective in hierarchical reinforcement learning, PyTrade uses an architectural hierarchy in which the SAA supplies signals to the hierarchical PAA. It does not implement temporal options or subgoal-based control \cite{Pateria2021}. The SAA is trained first on a single-asset task, frozen, and then used to generate per-asset signals that form part of the hierarchical PAA's observation. The hierarchical PAA remains the sole policy that executes the multi-asset allocation. Freezing the SAA isolates the transfer question because a hierarchical PAA outcome cannot be attributed to both a changing SAA and a changing hierarchical PAA at the same time. Section~\ref{sec:system-design} details the resulting POMDPs, feature pipeline, reward design, simulator, and out-of-sample evaluation.

The literature thus supports the individual choices of PPO, recurrent temporal state, and attention-based cross-sectional processing, but provides limited evidence on their controlled combination through a frozen signal-transfer interface. The contribution of this thesis is not to presume that the combination must dominate a monolithic model. Instead, it measures how the interface affects risk-adjusted performance, learning stability, turnover, drawdowns, and allocation behavior against matched ablations. That comparison makes it possible to distinguish a genuinely useful temporal signal from one that is redundant, harmful, or only conditionally useful in particular market regimes.
