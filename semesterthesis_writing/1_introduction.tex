\section{Introduction}

Learning to act optimally under uncertainty is one of the fundamental challenges in artificial intelligence \cite{SuttonBarto}. Few domains embody this challenge as clearly as global financial markets, where decisions are sequential, information is incomplete, and outcomes unfold over extended time horizons \cite{LopezdePrado.2018}. Deep reinforcement learning (DRL) has emerged as a promising approach for tackling sequential decision-making problems through learning from experiences. It allows a computer to understand a given problem as a hierarchy of simpler concepts and removes the need for a human operator to formally specify all the required knowledge \cite{Goodfellow-et-al-2016}. Financial return series can be characterized by their high volatility, low signal-to-noise ratio and raw prices series exhibit non-stationarity \cite{LopezdePrado.2018}, which further increases the challenges for successful DRL adoption. Within this setting, dynamic portfolio management involves adjusting portfolio exposures over time to balance returns, risk, transaction costs, and investor constraints, while accounting for the effects of each trade on cash, holdings, and future opportunities \cite{Sutiene2024}. Each asset in a portfolio can change its statistical relationship across market regimes, and a portfolio decision must jointly determine the direction and size of exposure \cite{LopezdePrado.2018, GabrielNixonRaj.2025}. Evidence for trend-following and time-series momentum motivates testing whether information from an asset’s own return history can complement a cross-sectional allocation rule \cite{Faber2007, Moskowitz2012}. Hierarchical reinforcement learning decomposes a complex decision problem into interacting subtasks in which lower-level policies provide representations or signals to a higher-level policy \cite{Pateria2021}. In this thesis, hierarchy refers specifically to the separation between single-asset signal generation and portfolio-level allocation, rather than to temporal options or subgoal-based control.

The relevant literature connects three modeling components that are commonly treated separately. Reinforcement learning formalizes repeated trading and rebalancing as decisions made under uncertainty and provides policy optimization methods such as proximal policy optimization (PPO) \cite{JohnSchulmanFilipWolskiPrafullaDhariwalAlecRadfordOlegKlimov.2017}; recurrent networks can encode information from previous observations, which may be useful when the current observation does not fully describe the relevant state \cite{Hochreiter1997, DBLP:journals/corr/HausknechtS15}; and self-attention provides a mechanism for comparing several asset representations at a common decision time \cite{vaswani2017}. Several recent financial-RL studies combine recurrent representation learning with policy optimization, while others compare recurrent and Transformer-based policies \cite{JunKevin.2025, Junfeng.2024, GabrielNixonRaj.2025}. The literature therefore supplies plausible components for a modular policy, but leaves their empirical interaction insufficiently resolved: evidence for recurrent financial policies, attention-based allocation, or alternative monolithic architectures does not establish the value of transferring a frozen, per-asset recurrent trading signal into a separate cross-sectional allocator \cite{JunKevin.2025, GabrielNixonRaj.2025, Xue.2026, Coulter2025}. The central research question is: \textit{How does transferring frozen recurrent single-asset signals to a self-attention portfolio allocator affect out-of-sample risk-adjusted performance, learning stability, and allocation behavior, compared to non-modular and signal-ablation baselines?}

PyTrade is the program developed for this thesis, and it makes this division explicit. A recurrent Single-Asset Agent (SAA) is trained on one asset at a time to turn its own feature history and position into a trading signal. A Portfolio Allocator Agent (PAA) then receives one such signal for every asset, together with the current cross-sectional market and portfolio state, and uses self-attention to choose portfolio weights for the whole asset universe. The SAA is frozen before PAA training, so the latter is intended to learn when its transferred temporal signals are useful, redundant, or misleading rather than changing the source policy to fit the allocation reward. This thesis investigates the research question with walk-forward, out-of-sample comparisons against matched ablations and baselines, so that temporal transfer, cross-sectional allocation, and their combination can be separated. This design makes a positive, negative, or conditional result informative: it can identify not only whether the architecture performs differently, but also under which market conditions and through which allocation choices the difference emerges. Chapter~2 presents the current state of research. Chapter~3 specifies the stationary, memory-preserving features, transaction-cost-aware simulator, PPO training procedure, and the information flow between both layers. The results are interpreted in chapter~4 through both portfolio outcomes and the mechanisms that produce them, including drawdowns, turnover, and the PAA's response to the transferred SAA information. Chapter~5 concludes and states the limitations of PyTrade.
