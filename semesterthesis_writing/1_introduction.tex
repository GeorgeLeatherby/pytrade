\section{Introduction}

Learning to act under uncertainty is a central challenge in artificial intelligence \cite{SuttonBarto}. Financial markets make this challenge concrete: decisions are sequential, information is incomplete, and outcomes unfold over time \cite{LopezdePrado.2018}. Reinforcement learning (RL) learns decisions from interactions scored by a reward; deep reinforcement learning (DRL) uses neural networks to represent the decision rule and related predictions \cite{SuttonBarto,Goodfellow-et-al-2016}. Its financial application is difficult because returns are noisy and volatile, while price levels are non-stationary: their statistical properties change over time \cite{LopezdePrado.2018}. Dynamic portfolio management must balance returns, risk, trading costs, and investor constraints while accounting for each trade's effect on cash, holdings, and future opportunities \cite{Sutiene2024}. Relationships between assets can also change across market regimes, periods with different volatility and co-movement patterns \cite{GabrielNixonRaj.2025}. Evidence for trend-following and time-series momentum motivates testing whether an asset's return history can complement cross-sectional allocation, the comparison of assets at one decision date \cite{Faber2007,Moskowitz2012}. Hierarchical reinforcement learning decomposes decisions into interacting subtasks \cite{Pateria2021}. Here, hierarchy separates single-asset signal generation from portfolio allocation; it does not use temporally extended actions or explicit subgoals.

The literature connects three relevant components. Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) updates a policy, a decision rule mapping observations to actions, while limiting incentives for large changes between updates \cite{JohnSchulmanFilipWolskiPrafullaDhariwalAlecRadfordOlegKlimov.2017}. Recurrent networks retain information from earlier observations \cite{Hochreiter1997,hausknecht2015deep}, while self-attention compares representations by weighting their relevance to one another \cite{vaswani2017}. Recent studies combine or compare these components \cite{JunKevin.2025,Junfeng.2024,GabrielNixonRaj.2025}, but do not establish the value of transferring a frozen recurrent trading signal into a separate allocator \cite{Xue.2026,Coulter2025}. The central research question is: \textit{How does transferring frozen recurrent single-asset signals to a self-attention portfolio allocator affect out-of-sample risk-adjusted performance, learning stability, and allocation behavior, compared with signal-free and randomized-signal baselines?} Out-of-sample evaluation uses periods excluded from parameter fitting; risk-adjusted performance considers returns alongside variability and losses.

PyTrade is the system developed for this thesis.\footnote{The source code and implementation details are available at \url{https://github.com/GeorgeLeatherby/pytrade}.} A recurrent Single-Asset Agent (SAA) learns to trade one asset at a time from its feature history and current position. A Portfolio Allocator Agent (PAA) receives a signal for every asset alongside market and portfolio information, then uses self-attention to choose allocation weights. Freezing the SAA means its parameters remain unchanged during allocator training. The PAA must learn when to use or discount the transferred signals. Purged block-level comparisons against matched ablations, which remove or replace the signal, test its contribution across historical regimes.

The principal passive reference is SPY, an exchange-traded fund (ETF) tracking the Standard \& Poor's 500 (S\&P 500) index of large United States companies.
