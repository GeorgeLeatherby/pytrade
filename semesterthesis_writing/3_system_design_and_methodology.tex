\section{System Design and Methodology}
\label{sec:system-design}

PyTrade separates temporal signal generation from cross-asset allocation. Sections~\ref{sec:simulator}, \ref{sec:universe}, and~\ref{sec:friction} first establish the simulator, asset universe, and trading costs shared by both agents. Section~\ref{sec:overview} then formalizes their decision problems. Sections~\ref{sec:state} and~\ref{sec:reward} describe the features and reward methodology, before Sections~\ref{sec:saa} and~\ref{sec:paa} explain the agents and their signal-transfer interface.

\subsection{Simulator Design}
\label{sec:simulator}

Both agents interact with one custom trading simulator. An episode begins by selecting a historical interval and initializing cash and holdings. Each trading-day step supplies an observation, executes the requested action at the current closing price, applies trading costs, advances to the next date, and returns a reward and updated account information. Historical prices are exogenous: the agent changes its portfolio, not the subsequent market-price path. The simulator therefore models execution costs rather than price formation caused by its own order flow.

\subsubsection{Execution Modes and Data Infrastructure}

The simulator supports single-asset position changes, portfolio-weight allocation, and two instruction-based execution modes for manual and rule-based tests. All modes share market data and the transaction-cost model. Separate shadow accounts allow frozen specialists to trade hypothetical portfolios without observing the allocator's live trades. This combination extends the requirements of standard single-policy trading environments, such as those provided by FinRL \cite{liu2022finrldeepreinforcementlearning}.

Market features are computed once and reused across episodes; account-dependent quantities are updated after each step. A rolling record retains prices, weights, returns, and risk statistics for observation and reward construction. Cash earns a trading-day carry derived from the Effective Federal Funds Rate (EFFR), an annualized measure of overnight unsecured lending between financial institutions. This is a modeled cash-return proxy, not a directly investable asset (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\subsubsection{Purged Training, Validation, and Test Blocks}

The available history is divided into repeated training and validation blocks, followed by one final test block:
\begin{equation}
[\text{train}_0][\text{purge}][\text{val}_0]\,[\text{train}_1][\text{purge}][\text{val}_1]\,\cdots\,[\text{purge}][\text{test}].
\end{equation}
Each training--validation pair reserves approximately \(20\%\) of its usable dates for validation, separated from training by a 60-trading-day purge. The final test also has a preceding purge. A purge is an interval excluded from both adjacent samples to reduce dependence through overlapping feature histories. During training a randomly starting consecutive 252 day episode is picked from a training block, meaning that each training episode remains entirely within one chosen block. This ensures that the available daily data is used evenly. Evaluation uses the fixed blocks described in Section~\ref{sec:oos-protocol}.

Features are calculated using only information available by the observation date. The purge exceeds the dominant 36-day normalization window, but does not formally cover the maximum 120-day fractional-differentiation lookback plus subsequent normalization (Section~\ref{sec:state-ffd}). However in \cite{LopezdePrado.2018} the impact of days from the second lookback half is examined to be small on the resulting feature. No additional purge is placed between a validation block and the following training block. Consequently, this is a purged historical block holdout, not strict walk-forward retraining in which every evaluated date follows all training dates.

The protocol follows the motivation for purged financial evaluation discussed by L\'opez de Prado \cite{LopezdePrado.2018}: serial dependence and overlapping histories can make shuffled validation misleading. Several historical validation blocks expose regime dependence, while the final test remains excluded from design and checkpoint selection. Full combinatorial purged cross-validation, which evaluates many combinations of held-out blocks, is not used because repeated complete policy training would exceed the computational budget.

\subsubsection{Hardware Placement and Batched Computation}

Environment execution runs on central processing units (CPUs), while neural-network calculations run on a graphics processing unit (GPU). CPUs suit branching account logic; a GPU processes large groups of similar matrix operations efficiently. With \(k\) parallel environments and \(N=11\) assets, the frozen specialist is evaluated over \(kN\) asset rows together, retaining separate recurrent states. The allocator processes the \(k\) observations as another batch. Figure~\ref{fig:sim-classes} summarizes this division.

\subsection{Asset Universe}
\label{sec:universe}

The simulator trades eleven series: SPY for United States equities; the country ETFs EWG, EWH, EWJ, EWQ, EWS, EWT, EWU, and EWY for Germany, Hong Kong, Japan, France, Singapore, Taiwan, the United Kingdom, and South Korea; and Crude oil and Gold futures. A futures contract agrees to exchange an asset at a specified future date, but these series use simplified directly held asset accounting rather than explicit margin, expiry, and rollover (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). The country funds are dollar-quoted iShares products. Common dollar denomination avoids an additional currency-conversion step; it does not remove foreign-currency exposure embedded in their underlying holdings.

Country equity funds and commodity series reduce dependence on individual companies and provide different economic exposures. Their purpose is cross-sectional diversity, not a predetermined relationship between selected pairs. Long daily histories support comparisons across regimes, including periods when equities move together and gold behaves differently. This universe defines the opportunities available to both agents; the following cost model determines the price of moving between them.

\subsection{Trading Frictions}
\label{sec:friction}

Every executed trade pays commission, half-spread, and a size-dependent impact charge, plus an optional fixed fee. Commission is a broker charge; the half-spread approximates the cost of trading across quoted bid and offer prices. Average daily dollar volume (ADV) measures recent liquidity. For traded notional \(n_j\), the monetary value of an order in asset \(j\), total transaction cost is
\begin{equation}
\text{Cost}_t = c_{\text{comm}}\sum_j n_j + c_{\text{spread}}\sum_j n_j
+ \lambda_{\text{impact}}\sum_j n_j\sqrt{\frac{n_j}{\text{ADV}_j}}
+ f_{\text{fixed}}\cdot\#\{\text{orders}\}.
\end{equation}
The impact term increases the per-dollar cost as an order becomes large relative to available liquidity. Its square-root dependence is sublinear per dollar, although total impact cost grows as \(n_j^{3/2}\); it should not be confused with a linear trading fee. The coefficients and liquidity window are fixed for each experiment. Setting the charges to zero permits a frictionless comparison, whereas the reported evaluations use configured costs. Together, simulator execution, the asset universe, and these frictions define the decision problem formalized next.

\subsection{System Overview}
\label{sec:overview}

The SAA learns position changes for one asset, while the PAA allocates capital across all eleven. The specialist is trained first and frozen; the allocator then receives its per-asset actions alongside market and account information. The allocator can use, discount, or override these signals. This separates temporal representation from cross-sectional allocation without assuming that transfer must improve performance.

At each trading day, the observation supplies market features and account information. The policy emits a position change or allocation request; the simulator executes it, revalues the portfolio, and scores the result. PPO updates network parameters from batches of these trajectories. Episodes differ in their historical intervals and initial accounts, but replayed prices do not respond to the agent's trades.

Splitting the temporal problem from the cross-sectional problem is a direct architectural response to two very different structures in the underlying decision problem, not merely a modeling convenience. Learning a good trading rule for one asset from its own price history has a strong sequential-memory requirement: an unusual move today only makes sense read against the last weeks of context, which recurrent networks such as the Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) cell used here are specifically built to carry forward. Learning how eleven assets should be weighted \emph{against each other}, by contrast, is at every instant a set problem rather than a sequence problem: given today's picture of all eleven assets, the right relative allocation does not fundamentally depend on the order in which the assets happen to be listed, which is exactly the permutation-respecting structure a self-attention mechanism (Section~\ref{sec:paa}) provides and a recurrent network does not. Asking one, monolithic network to learn both structures at once would force it to discover this decomposition on its own, from a single reward signal alone; PyTrade instead builds the decomposition directly into the architecture, training a temporal specialist first and a cross-sectional specialist second, and only asking the second network to learn the comparatively narrower problem of how much to trust the first.

We formalize both layers' decision problems with the same general tuple and instantiate it twice, with agent-specific state, action, and observation spaces, in Sections~\ref{sec:saa} and~\ref{sec:paa}. A Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP) is a tuple

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{M} = (\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, \mathcal{O}, \mathcal{P}, \mathcal{R}, \mathcal{Z}),
\end{equation}

Here $\mathcal{S}$ is the simulator state, $\mathcal{A}$ the available actions, $\mathcal{O}$ the observations, $\mathcal{P}$ the transition rule, $\mathcal{R}$ the reward, and $\mathcal{Z}$ the map from state to observation. Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE) combines value-prediction errors across time to estimate how an action performed relative to expectation. The discount factor $\gamma\in[0.98,0.998]$ reduces the weight of later rewards; the GAE decay parameter $\lambda\in[0.95,0.98]$ controls reliance on longer trajectories. The finite episode horizon does not require discounting, but these settings provide a bias--variance trade-off during learning.


Partial observability arises because daily prices and engineered features omit market-relevant information, including news, fundamentals, and order-book conditions. The SAA's recurrent state summarizes past observations; it is a response to this information gap, not its cause. The PAA has no recurrent state of its own, but receives historical context through engineered features, running account statistics, and frozen SAA signals. A memoryless policy does not make the observation fully Markovian.

\subsection{State Representation: A Shared Feature Engineering Pipeline}
\label{sec:state}

Raw price levels are generally non-stationary; adding more history often worsens the changing scale. A weakly \emph{stationary} process has a constant mean and variance, with covariance depending on the lag rather than calendar time. Features whose typical scale changes between training and evaluation can weaken a learned decision rule. The enrichment pipeline therefore transforms OHLCV into more stable, comparable quantities. Market features are computed offline and reused; cash, weights, costs, and other action-dependent portfolio quantities are updated each step.

Two transformations recur. Log returns, $r_t^{(N)}=\log(P_t/P_{t-N})$, add across time and represent proportional rather than dollar changes. They still record the net loss from a \(+10\%\) move followed by \(-10\%\); additivity does not cancel that loss. Rolling $z$-score standardization expresses a quantity relative to its recent mean and standard deviation:
\begin{equation}
\hat x_t = \frac{x_t - \mu_w(x)_t}{\sigma_w(x)_t + \epsilon},
\end{equation}
where $\mu_w$ and $\sigma_w$ are trailing-window statistics and $\epsilon$ prevents division by zero. Most normalization windows span 36 trading days, none longer then 60 to honor the purge window. Some standardized quantities also pass through the hyperbolic tangent, $\tanh$, which bounds them between \(-1\) and \(1\). The same transformations are applied across assets, while each experiment fixes which features reach the specialist (SAA) and the allocator's (PAA) asset and portfolio tokens (Sections~\ref{sec:saa} and~\ref{sec:paa}).

\subsubsection{Multi-Horizon Return, Volume, and Volatility Features}
\label{sec:state-returns}

A common feature family is computed at $N\in\{1,5,21\}$ trading days, representing daily, weekly, and approximately monthly horizons. For each horizon:

\begin{equation}
r_t^{(N)} = \log\frac{P_t}{P_{t-N}}, \qquad r_t^{(N),\text{SPY}} = \log\frac{P_t^{\text{SPY}}}{P_{t-N}^{\text{SPY}}}, \qquad \tilde{r}_t^{(N)} = r_t^{(N)} - r_t^{(N),\text{SPY}},
\end{equation}

These are the asset return, SPY return, and their difference. The asset's raw log return is retained alongside a standardized market-relative return:

\begin{equation}
z_{\mathrm{rel},t}^{(N)} = \text{clip}\!\left(\frac{\tilde{r}_t^{(N)} - \mu_{36}(\tilde{r}^{(N)})_t}{\sigma_{36}(\tilde{r}^{(N)})_t + \epsilon}, \, -4, 4\right),
\end{equation}

where clipping limits extreme observations to \([-4,4]\). Subtracting SPY provides a view of asset-specific movement relative to the market, while the retained raw return still supplies broad directional information.

A volatility-breakout companion compares short-term variability with the recent relative-return scale:

\begin{equation}
z_{\mathrm{vol},t}^{(N)} = \text{clip}\!\left(\frac{\log\big(\sigma_5(\tilde{r}^{(N)})_t + \epsilon\big) - \mu_{36}(\tilde{r}^{(N)})_t}{\sigma_{36}(\tilde{r}^{(N)})_t + \epsilon}, \, -4, 4\right),
\end{equation}

using the log of a five-day volatility estimate and the trailing 36-day relative-return mean and standard deviation. Volume intensity similarly compares current volume with its trailing average through a log ratio. The feature set retains that intensity and a market-relative version obtained by subtracting SPY's intensity, standardizing, and applying \(\tanh\). These quantities distinguish unusually active trading from price movement alone.

\subsubsection{Intraday Shape and Classical Technical Indicators}

A second group describes the daily price bar, the session's open, high, low, and close. It records where the close falls within the day's range and whether the opening price differs from the previous close.

\begin{equation}
\text{intra\_vol}_t = \log\frac{H_t}{L_t}, \qquad
\text{cl\_strength}_t = \frac{C_t - L_t}{H_t - L_t}, \qquad
\text{gap}_t = \log\frac{O_t}{C_{t-1}}, \qquad
\text{shadow}_t = \frac{H_t - \max(O_t, C_t)}{H_t - L_t},
\end{equation}

These measure the log high--low range, closing strength, overnight gap, and upper wick (the part of the range above both open and close). The range and gap are standardized and bounded with \(\tanh\). Closing strength and upper-wick fraction already lie in \([0,1]\) and are rescaled to \([-1,1]\).

Three classical technical indicators are computed alongside these bar-shape features, standard tools in discretionary trading practice that are worth defining explicitly since they are rarely discussed in the reinforcement-learning literature, and each rescaled here to be dimensionless and scale-free across the eleven-asset universe.

The \emph{Relative Strength Index} (RSI) compares average gains with average losses over 14 trading days. Its gain-to-loss ratio, called relative strength (RS), gives \(\mathrm{RSI}=100-100/(1+\mathrm{RS})\). Values above 70 and below 30 are conventionally described as overbought and oversold, respectively, not as guarantees of a reversal. The indicator is recentered to \([-1,1]\) and smoothed over three days.

A \emph{Bollinger Band} surrounds a rolling mean price with an envelope at a chosen multiple of standard deviation. Here the feature is relative band width, \(4\sigma_{36}(P)_t/\mu_{36}(P)_t\), rather than the boundaries or price position. It is standardized and clipped to \([-3,3]\). Widening indicates greater recent variability; narrowing indicates compression. Dividing by mean price makes the measure comparable across differently priced instruments.

The \emph{Average True Range} (ATR) averages \(\max(H_t-L_t,|H_t-C_{t-1}|,|L_t-C_{t-1}|)\), including opening gaps that the high--low range can miss. It is standardized over 36 days and clipped to \([-3,3]\). Together, these indicators summarize momentum, relative price variability, and gap-aware volatility beyond the return and volume features.

\subsubsection{Cross-Asset Features}

Three features make each asset's representation depend explicitly on the rest of the universe, giving the PAA's cross-sectional attention (Section~\ref{sec:paa}) genuine correlation structure to work with even before any learned attention weights are applied.

The first is a rolling 21-day Pearson correlation, the standard linear co-movement statistic 
\begin{equation}
    \rho = \mathrm{Cov}(r^{(1)},r^{(1),\text{SPY}})/\big(\sigma(r^{(1)})\,\sigma(r^{(1),\text{SPY}})\big),
\end{equation}
between the asset's daily log return and SPY's. Correlation coefficients are bounded in $(-1,1)$ and their sampling distribution is skewed near those bounds, which makes rolling $z$-scoring of the raw coefficient statistically awkward; the \emph{Fisher $z$-transform} addresses this directly,

\begin{equation}
\rho_t = \text{clip}\big(\text{Corr}_{21}(r^{(1)}, r^{(1),\text{SPY}})_t,\, -0.99, 0.99\big), \qquad
z\text{\_fisher\_corr\_spy}_t = \tfrac{1}{2}\log\frac{1+\rho_t}{1-\rho_t},
\end{equation}

which maps the bounded correlation onto an unbounded, approximately variance-stabilized scale; this is what makes it safe to treat like any other roughly Gaussian feature within this pipeline's shared $z$-score/$\tanh$ normalization scheme, rather than a bounded ratio whose variance changes depending on how close it already sits to $\pm 1$.

The second is rolling 36-day \emph{beta}, \(\beta=\mathrm{Cov}(r^{(1)},r^{(1),\mathrm{SPY}})/\mathrm{Var}(r^{(1),\mathrm{SPY}})\), measuring sensitivity to SPY's returns. This is the covariance-to-market-variance ratio used in a capital asset pricing model regression. It is standardized over 36 days; beta above one indicates greater historical sensitivity than the market.

The third is \emph{dispersion}: the log standard deviation of daily returns across the eleven assets, standardized over 36 days. It gives every token a common measure of how differently assets are moving. High dispersion indicates greater cross-sectional variation, not necessarily an exploitable opportunity.

\subsubsection{Memory-Preserving Stationary Features via Fractional Differentiation}
\label{sec:state-ffd}

Ordinary differencing removes price-level information and can weaken slow-moving trend context. Fractional differentiation instead combines log prices with gradually decaying weights governed by a non-integer order \(d\), retaining some historical contribution while reducing non-stationarity \cite{LopezdePrado.2018}. Smaller orders preserve more memory but may leave stronger non-stationarity. Figure~\ref{fig:ffd-pipeline} summarizes the fixed-width approximation and its empirical checks.

The search tests orders from \(0.45\) to \(0.8\) in increments of \(0.05\), using SPY, Gold, and Crude as anchor assets. The Augmented Dickey--Fuller (ADF) test assesses the null hypothesis of a unit root, a persistent random-walk component; \(p<0.05\) rejects that null at the chosen significance level. It is evidence against this form of non-stationarity, not proof of stationarity under every definition. The largest of the anchors' smallest passing orders is applied across assets so their transformation is comparable. Weights are truncated below \(10^{-3}\) or at a 120-day cap. The transformed series is standardized over 36 days, bounded with \(\tanh\), and checked for all eleven assets; every series must reject the unit-root null before the feature is used.

\subsubsection{Calendar Encoding}

A final block encodes day of week, relative trading-day position within the month, and month of year as six calendar features. A sine--cosine pair represents each cycle without an artificial jump at its boundary:

\begin{equation}
x^{\sin}_t = \sin\!\Big(\tfrac{2\pi\, k_t}{K}\Big), \qquad x^{\cos}_t = \cos\!\Big(\tfrac{2\pi\, k_t}{K}\Big),
\end{equation}

with \((k_t,K)\) representing the position and length of the relevant cycle: five trading weekdays, the month's trading days, or twelve months. This makes adjacent cycle endpoints close in feature space, unlike an ordinary integer index.

Feature selection is fixed separately for the specialist, asset tokens, and portfolio token in each experiment. This separates changes in available information from changes in the neural architecture and supports the feature ablation reported in Chapter~4.

\subsection{A Shared Reward Design Methodology}
\label{sec:reward}

Both layers are trained with the same reward methodology, applied to two different notions of ``portfolio'': the SAA's private cash-plus-one-asset sub-portfolio (Section~\ref{sec:saa}), and the PAA's actual, live, eleven-asset portfolio (Section~\ref{sec:paa}). The design goal throughout is to reward risk-adjusted skill, not exposure to a rising market, and to do so with a reward that is \emph{dense}: computed from directly observable portfolio and market quantities at every single trading day, rather than only once at the end of an episode. Density matters specifically for PPO's mechanics: the algorithm's advantage estimates (Section~\ref{sec:saa}) are built from a value function trained to predict returns-to-go from every intermediate state, and a reward that stays silent for 251 of 252 steps and fires only at the very end would give that value function almost nothing to regress against until very late in training.

\paragraph{Multi-Objective Reward Composition.}

The general reward combines excess return, changes in downside-adjusted performance, drawdown penalties, and an execution-gap term. Drawdown (DD) denotes a fall from a previous portfolio-value peak. Coefficients determine which components are active; the formula describes available mechanisms, not five necessarily nonzero penalties in every experiment:

\begin{equation}
R_t^{\text{raw}} = \underbrace{s_\alpha \, \Delta\ell_t}_{\text{excess return}} \;+\; \underbrace{s_\sigma \, \Delta\text{Sortino}_t}_{\text{risk-adjusted trend}} \;-\; \underbrace{\lambda_{dd}\,\Delta\text{DD}_t^{+}}_{\text{drawdown shock}} \;-\; \underbrace{\lambda_{dd}^{\text{lvl}}\,\text{DD}_t^{\text{level}}}_{\text{sustained drawdown}} \;-\; \underbrace{\lambda_{\text{exec}}\,G_t}_{\text{execution gap}}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
R_t = \tanh\!\Big(\frac{R_t^{\text{raw}}}{\tau}\Big) \cdot \sigma_R.
\end{equation}

The hyperbolic tangent bounds the observed reward to $(-\sigma_R,\sigma_R)$. The divisor $\tau$ controls saturation and $\sigma_R$ controls output scale. Both agents share this methodology but use different passive return counterfactuals. In the selected allocator configuration, persistent drawdown and execution-gap coefficients are zero; these mechanisms therefore cannot explain its results.

\paragraph{Excess Return over a Passive Counterfactual.}
\label{sec:excess-return}

Rewarding raw portfolio return teaches an agent that ``the market goes up'' is a strategy, which is not the behavior this thesis is trying to elicit or measure. Both agents are instead rewarded relative to a passive counterfactual portfolio started with the same capital. Writing $V_t$ for the live (SAA: single-asset sub-) portfolio value and $B_t$ for the counterfactual's value,

\begin{equation}
\Delta\ell_t = \big(\log V_t - \log V_{t-1}\big) - \big(\log B_t - \log B_{t-1}\big).
\end{equation}

Because both terms are log-returns, $\Delta\ell_t$ is the log \emph{excess return} of the agent over ``having done nothing,'' which isolates timing and selection skill from beta exposure to a rising market. The concrete choice of counterfactual $B_t$ differs by agent: the SAA is scored against the buy-and-hold return of the one asset it happens to be trading, while the PAA is scored against a fully invested SPY buy-and-hold. Sections~\ref{sec:saa} and~\ref{sec:paa} give the exact construction of each counterfactual, since in the PAA's case there are two further diagnostic-only portfolios in the codebase that are easy to confuse with the actual reward counterfactual.

\paragraph{Risk-Adjusted Trend: The Differential Sortino Ratio.}

An exponential moving average (EMA) updates a running estimate by blending in each new observation with a fixed weight $\eta$ and discounting all older observations geometrically, $\bar x_t = \bar x_{t-1} + \eta(x_t - \bar x_{t-1})$; unlike a simple moving average over a fixed window, it needs no buffer of raw history and reacts immediately to a regime change, at the cost of a memory that fades smoothly rather than truncating sharply at a window edge. Two such EMAs track the mean step return and the downside variance (squared negative returns only, floored to avoid division blow-ups on a run that has had no losing steps yet):

\begin{equation}
\bar{r}_t = \bar{r}_{t-1} + \eta\big(r_t - \bar{r}_{t-1}\big), \qquad
\bar{v}_t^{-} = \bar{v}_{t-1}^{-} + \eta\Big(\min(r_t,0)^2 - \bar{v}_{t-1}^{-}\Big),
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\text{Sortino}_t = \frac{\bar{r}_t}{\sqrt{\max(\bar{v}_t^{-}, v_{\min})}}, \qquad
\Delta\text{Sortino}_t = \text{Sortino}_t - \text{Sortino}_{t-1}.
\end{equation}

The classical Sharpe ratio scores a strategy by its mean return divided by the standard deviation of \emph{all} returns, penalizing upside volatility exactly as much as downside volatility; an investor who welcomes large gains and fears only large losses is arguably better served by the Sortino ratio above, which keeps the same numerator but replaces total volatility in the denominator with \emph{downside deviation}, the standard deviation computed only over return periods that were actually negative. Rewarding the \emph{change} in this ratio, rather than its level, gives a step-wise, differentiable signal that still points the policy toward a smoother, higher-Sortino equity curve, without waiting for episode termination to score risk. A short warm-up window suppresses this term until the EMAs have enough history to be meaningful, avoiding an artificial spike from the zero-initialized baseline.

\paragraph{Drawdown Penalties.}
\label{sec:drawdown}

A \emph{drawdown} at time $t$ is the percentage decline of the current portfolio value from its own running historical peak, $\text{DD}_t = \max\big(0,\, 1 - V_t / \text{Peak}_t\big)$: the single most common risk metric in practitioner risk reporting, since, unlike volatility, it directly answers the question an investor actually asks, how much of my money was at risk at the worst point so far. Two complementary drawdown terms are used here. An incremental penalty charges only the \emph{new} drawdown incurred this step, $\Delta\text{DD}_t^{+} = \max(0, \text{DD}_t - \text{DD}_{t-1})$, over a growing lookback window (starting at 2 steps and expanding to a configured cap), so early-episode noise cannot trigger a large penalty. A persistent level penalty additionally charges every step spent underwater relative to the running peak, $\text{DD}_t^{\text{level}} = \max\big(0,\, 1 - V_t / \text{Peak}_t\big)$, discouraging the agent from lingering in a drawdown even once it has stopped worsening.

\paragraph{The Execution Gap.}
\label{sec:execgap}

The execution-gap term penalizes the distance between what the policy asked for and what the environment actually filled, net of a deadband that excludes changes too small to trade profitably; its purpose is to keep the policy honest about the cost of the requests it makes, without punishing it for a request the environment itself declined to fill because available cash or inventory was insufficient, a mechanically infeasible request rather than a strategic one. For the PAA, this is the vector gap left by the soft-execution mechanics of Section~\ref{sec:paa}:

\begin{equation}
G_t^{\text{paa}} = \sum_{j} \max\big(0,\, \lvert \Delta w_t^{(j)} - \Delta w_t^{\text{exec},(j)} \rvert - \delta\big).
\end{equation}

For the SAA, the same idea applies to the scalar action: writing $a_t^{\text{exec}}$ for the fraction of portfolio value actually traded (which can fall short of $a_t$ if cash or inventory is insufficient), the gap is

\begin{equation}
G_t^{\text{saa}} = \max\big(0,\, \lvert a_t - a_t^{\text{exec}} \rvert - g_0\big),
\end{equation}

where $g_0$ is a dynamic no-penalty band derived from the minimum trade-value threshold so that trades too small to clear the transaction-cost floor are never penalized as an execution failure. Both terms use an asymmetric multiplier, $\lambda_{\text{exec}}$ scaled up when net exposure was requested but not delivered, scaled down when the policy over-shoots and the environment simply declines to execute more than requested, without punishing either agent for the deliberate execution friction built into its own action mechanics (Sections~\ref{sec:saa} and~\ref{sec:paa}).


\subsection{Layer 1: The Single-Asset Agent}
\label{sec:saa}

The SAA is the temporal specialist. Each training episode randomly assigns one asset, and the agent decides whether to increase, reduce, or retain its position each step. A two-layer LSTM carries a learned summary across trading days. Market-relative features provide context, but the agent cannot move its capital to another instrument within the episode.

\subsubsection{State, Observation, and Action Space}

At each step $t$ the SAA holds a position in exactly one, randomly selected asset per episode. The relevant slice of simulator state is

\begin{equation}
s_t^{\text{saa}} = \big(p_t,\, c_t,\, x_t\big),
\end{equation}

the asset's close price $p_t$, the cash balance $c_t$, and the held share count $x_t$. Writing the state this way, rather than listing everything the simulator internally tracks, isolates the \emph{endogenous} part of $\mathcal{S}$ for this agent: $c_t$ and $x_t$ are exactly the two quantities the SAA's own trades change, and $p_t$ is the one exogenous quantity its valuation and execution depend on at every step. The much larger, purely exogenous remainder of $\mathcal{S}$, the historical price and volume panel each feature of Section~\ref{sec:state} is computed from, evolves independently of the agent and is left implicit here; it still enters $\mathcal{Z}$ below, just never through anything the SAA's actions can influence. Decomposing state this way, an account state (cash, holdings) alongside a separately-described market state, is standard practice in algorithmic-trading reinforcement learning, matching, for instance, FinRL's own state definition \cite{liu2022finrldeepreinforcementlearning}. Making $s_t^{\text{saa}}$ explicit before the observation grounds the abstract POMDP tuple of Section~\ref{sec:overview} in a concrete instance, answering ``what does $\mathcal{S}$ actually contain here'' before $\mathcal{Z}$ decides what of it the network is allowed to see.

The observation $o_t^{\text{saa}} = \mathcal{Z}(s_t^{\text{saa}})$ handed to the policy is the concatenation of three blocks:


\begin{equation}
o_t^{\text{saa}} = \big[\, \mathbf{f}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{32},\ \ \mathbf{m}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{6},\ \ \mathbf{e}_i \in \{0,1\}^{11} \,\big],
\end{equation}

where $\mathbf{f}_t$ is the SAA's selected subset of the shared feature pipeline (Section~\ref{sec:state}), here the 26 market-derived indicators plus the 6 calendar features, $\mathbf{e}_i$ is a one-hot encoding of which of the $N{=}11$ universe assets is currently traded, and $\mathbf{m}_t$ is a portfolio memory block,

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{m}_t = \Big(\log\tfrac{c_t}{c_0},\ \log\tfrac{x_t p_t}{c_0},\ r_t^{\text{sub}},\ a_{t-1},\ z_t^{\text{rf}},\ \alpha_t^{\text{rf}}\Big).
\end{equation}

The first two entries express cash and held-asset value relative to initial capital $c_0$. The next two record the previous account return and action. The final entries provide a 60-day standardized cash-rate measure and account excess return over cash carry. These inputs connect the recurrent summary to the agent's current account rather than requiring the network to reconstruct its holdings from history alone.

The action space is a single continuous scalar, $\mathcal{A}_{\text{saa}} = [-1, 1]$, interpreted not as a target weight but as a \emph{target position change}: a fixed fraction of portfolio notional, the action-limiting factor $\kappa_t$, is annealed over training via a three-phase linear schedule (constant at a warm-up value, linear ramp, constant at a terminal value), and $a_t = 1$ requests a buy of $1 \cdot \kappa_t \cdot c_0$ dollars, subject to available cash, while $a_t = -0.5$ requests a sell of $0.5\,\kappa_t \cdot c_0$ dollars, subject to available inventory. This design keeps the action distribution centered and bounded regardless of how large the current position already is, which matters concretely because the same scalar head, unmodified, is reused across every asset once the SAA is frozen and copied (Section~\ref{sec:paa}): a head that instead output an absolute target weight would need to already know the notional scale of whichever asset it happened to be controlling. The environment executes the implied trade, marks the sub-portfolio to market, and returns a reward that instantiates the general formula of Section~\ref{sec:reward} against a same-asset, transaction-cost-paying buy-and-hold counterfactual.

\subsubsection{Network Architecture}

An asset identifier is represented by a one-hot vector, with one entry equal to one and the others zero, then mapped to a six-dimensional learned embedding. Market and account features join this embedding before a multilayer perceptron (MLP), a feed-forward network of dense layers. Its blocks apply layer normalization, which rescales intermediate features, a sigmoid linear unit (SiLU) activation, which introduces a smooth nonlinear transformation, and dropout, which randomly suppresses intermediate units during training. Figure~\ref{fig:saa-arch} shows the subsequent recurrent and output layers.

\label{sec:actor-critic-theory}
The critic provides a baseline for judging whether an action performed better than expected; it does not supply part of the executed action. Actor and critic can influence shared representations during training, but only the actor's action distribution is used for trading.

\subsubsection{Recurrent Memory and Advantage Estimation}

Two pieces of PPO vocabulary the literature review introduced only in passing are worth fixing precisely before describing how the SAA's two recursions interact. The \emph{advantage} $\hat A_t = \hat R_t - V(s_t)$ compares an empirical return-to-go estimate $\hat R_t$ against the critic's own prediction $V(s_t)$ (Section~\ref{sec:actor-critic-theory}); a positive advantage means the action taken at step $t$ did better than the critic expected, and PPO's clipped surrogate objective pushes the policy to make such actions more likely going forward. \emph{Generalized Advantage Estimation} (GAE), introduced alongside PPO, computes $\hat A_t$ not from a single one-step temporal-difference residual $\delta_t = r_t + \gamma V(s_{t+1}) - V(s_t)$, but from an exponentially-weighted average of $\delta_t, \delta_{t+1}, \delta_{t+2}, \dots$ controlled by a decay parameter $\lambda$, trading off bias (small $\lambda$, leaning on a critic that may itself be miscalibrated) against variance (large $\lambda$, leaning on longer, noisier realized trajectories). \emph{Backpropagation-through-time} (BPTT) is the mechanism by which a recurrent network's gradient at step $t$ is computed by unrolling the recurrence and differentiating through every preceding step the current hidden state depends on; the longer the unroll, the more expensive the computation and the more prone it is to vanishing or exploding gradients, which is why practical recurrent training truncates it to a bounded window.

Recurrent memory and GAE serve different purposes. The LSTM carries observation history during trajectory collection. GAE then works backward through the collected rewards and value estimates, using $A_t=\delta_t+\gamma\lambda A_{t+1}$ and stopping propagation at episode boundaries. It supplies training targets reflecting outcomes beyond the immediate reward.

Optimization replays bounded trajectory segments with the appropriate recurrent starting states. Backpropagation through time is therefore limited by the available sequence segment, whereas the advantage targets have already been calculated over the collected rollout. This separates recurrent gradient propagation from reward attribution; truncation controls computational cost but can still affect what temporal relationships are learned.

\subsubsection{Training Dynamics and Stability}
\label{sec:saa-stability}

Recurrent policy training balances exploration against convergence. The learning rate, entropy coefficient, clipping interval, and action-limiting factor follow hold--ramp--hold schedules. Entropy measures uncertainty in the action distribution; weighting it encourages exploration rather than premature commitment to one behavior. Gradually adjusting these settings limits rapid policy collapse and oscillatory trading. A separate transfer risk arises if the frozen specialist observes accounts changed by the allocator's trades. Shadow portfolios prevent this direct interference (Section~\ref{sec:info-flow}).

\subsection{Layer 2: The Portfolio Allocator Agent}
\label{sec:paa}

The PAA is the system's cross-sectional specialist. Rather than learning its own opinion about each asset's timing from scratch, it consumes a frozen copy of the SAA just described, one instance per asset, as a feature, and learns how to weigh that opinion against raw market data and against every other asset's own opinion, using a self-attention mechanism (already introduced conceptually in the literature review) instead of a recurrent one. This section proceeds in the same order the PAA's information actually flows: how its observation is built from per-asset tokens (Section~\ref{sec:paa-state}), how the frozen SAA copies are integrated into that observation through a shadow-portfolio mechanism (Section~\ref{sec:info-flow}), how its action is executed (Section~\ref{sec:paa-action}), and finally how its network processes all of this end to end (Section~\ref{sec:paa-network}).

\subsubsection{State Space: Tokenizing the Asset and Portfolio Universe}
\label{sec:paa-state}

The PAA observes the full eleven-asset universe at once, rather than one asset at a time. Its observation is a sequence of $N+1$ tokens, one embedding vector per asset plus one portfolio-level token summarizing the live, actually-traded portfolio:

\begin{equation}
o_t^{\text{paa}} = \big(\mathbf{T}^{(1)}_t,\, \dots,\, \mathbf{T}^{(N)}_t,\, \mathbf{T}^{\text{pf}}_t\big), \qquad \mathbf{T}^{(i)}_t, \mathbf{T}^{\text{pf}}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}}}.
\end{equation}

The shared embedding width is $d_{\text{model}}=64$, following standard Transformer notation \cite{vaswani2017}. Asset and portfolio tokens contain different raw information; separate learned projections map them into the common space before attention compares them.

Each full-feature asset token has 32 raw inputs: 26 market features and six account or policy quantities. The latter are the specialist's current scaled action, its shadow holding fraction, the live allocator weight, the allocator's recorded target weight, and the shadow account's Sortino and drawdown summaries. The market features comprise five return, volatility, and volume quantities at each of three horizons, plus four price-bar measures, three classical indicators, correlation, beta, dispersion, and the fractionally differentiated price feature (Section~\ref{sec:state}). Four of the six added quantities describe the SAA's shadow account; the other two describe the live allocator's position and request.

The portfolio token has 65 raw inputs: six calendar features, twelve live weights, fourteen scalar account diagnostics, and three eleven-entry vectors containing recorded allocator targets, shadow Sortino summaries, and shadow drawdowns. The scalars comprise daily simple-return excess, rolling Sharpe, drawdown change, recent return variability, weight-change turnover, concentration, current and recorded previous Sortino, moving-average return and downside state, previous maximum drawdown, a standardized cash rate, portfolio excess log return over cash, and the daily cash rate. Observation turnover is half the summed absolute risky-weight changes, not the executed-notional turnover reported in Chapter~4.

The concentration diagnostic is the reciprocal sum of squared live risky weights, divided by eleven. The risky weights are not renormalized after excluding cash: one corresponds to a fully invested equal-weight portfolio, but cash holdings can make the value exceed one. It is therefore not a bounded diversification score. The portfolio token mixes live diagnostics with shadow risk information, rather than being restricted to live-account quantities.

The PAA recomputes its decision from the current tokens and has no recurrent state. Historical information arrives through engineered features, specialist signals, and running account statistics. Its feed-forward attention operates across the current \(N+1\) tokens, not across a sequence of past dates.

\subsubsection{Integrating the Single-Asset Agent: Shadow Portfolios}
\label{sec:info-flow}

Section~\ref{sec:saa-stability} identified a structural risk in reusing one trained SAA across eleven assets: if each copy's LSTM hidden state were updated from the \emph{live}, PAA-controlled portfolio, that hidden state would drift out of the distribution it was trained on the moment the PAA starts making allocation decisions the SAA never saw during single-asset training, an exogenous rebalance that, from the SAA's own perspective, looks like a domain shift it was never trained to handle. PyTrade avoids this risk entirely, by construction rather than by regularization, using the shadow-portfolio mechanism shown in Figure~\ref{fig:shadow-flow}.

One frozen specialist policy is evaluated across the assets as a batch, with a separate recurrent state and hypothetical account for each asset. Its observations use that account's cash and holdings, never the live allocator account. The deterministic action, rescaled by the specialist's training action limit, updates the shadow account and enters the asset token. The shadow holding fraction is

\begin{equation}
\rho_t^{(i)} = \frac{\text{shadow shares}_i \cdot \text{price}_i}{\text{shadow cash}_i + \text{shadow shares}_i \cdot \text{price}_i},
\end{equation}

This fraction is computed from the isolated shadow account, unlike the live allocator weight. Two further shadow summaries describe downside-adjusted performance and drawdown. The first uses exponentially weighted mean and downside variability of the environment's shadow-return proxy, with a denominator floor and clipping to \([-3,3]\). That proxy combines the asset-notional change with cash before carry minus cash after carry, divided by prior account value. Cash carry therefore enters with the opposite sign from an ordinary net account return; the resulting input should not be treated as a conventional investment Sortino ratio. The drawdown summary instead measures the largest decline in shadow account value within a growing, capped window. Both enter the asset and portfolio tokens as environment-maintained summaries, not extra outputs from the frozen neural policy.

This isolation prevents allocator trades from directly changing the specialist's account inputs or recurrent state. It preserves single-asset accounting, but does not guarantee identical observation distributions across initialization, normalization, or market conditions. The PAA can discount the action, holding fraction, and shadow risk summaries. It does not receive the specialist's recurrent hidden state or an explicit uncertainty estimate. Figure~\ref{fig:shadow-flow} shows the transfer boundary.

\subsubsection{Action Space: Softmax Allocation with a Fixed Cash Anchor}
\label{sec:paa-action}

The PAA's actor head outputs $N$ real-valued logits $\mathbf{z}_t \in \mathbb{R}^N$, one per risky (non-cash) asset, $\mathcal{A}_{\text{paa}} = \mathbb{R}^N$. A cash logit is fixed at $z_t^{(0)} \equiv 0$ and prepended, and the executed target weights follow from a stable softmax, the standard mapping from an unconstrained real-valued vector onto the probability simplex (all entries non-negative, summing to one):

\begin{equation}
w_t^{(j)} = \frac{\exp\big(z_t^{(j)} - \max_k z_t^{(k)}\big)}{\sum_{k=0}^{N} \exp\big(z_t^{(k)} - \max_k z_t^{(k)}\big)}, \qquad j = 0, \dots, N.
\end{equation}

Anchoring the cash logit at a fixed value, rather than learning it, removes one redundant degree of freedom from the policy: softmax is shift-invariant, $\text{softmax}(\mathbf{z}) = \text{softmax}(\mathbf{z}+c)$ for any constant $c$, so an unconstrained, learned cash logit would only ever matter relative to the asset logits and could always be re-absorbed into them, meaning the network would have to learn to keep it at some implicit reference value anyway. Fixing it at zero removes that redundancy outright and gives the agent a well-defined, always-available ``do nothing'' reference point that does not have to be rediscovered from scratch at every stage of training.

The resulting target weights are not applied instantaneously. A soft-execution step moves the live portfolio only part-way toward the target,

\begin{equation}
\Delta w_t = w_t - w_t^{\text{live}}, \qquad
\Delta w_t^{\text{exec}} = \eta_{\text{exec}} \cdot \Delta w_t \cdot \mathbb{1}\big[\lvert \Delta w_t \rvert > \delta\big],
\end{equation}

with step size $\eta_{\text{exec}} \in (0,1]$ and deadband $\delta$. The deadband suppresses trades too small to be worth the transaction cost (Section~\ref{sec:friction}), and the partial step spreads a large rebalance over several days rather than realizing it as one trade, which is closer to how a real allocator would rebalance and keeps single-step turnover, and therefore cost, bounded. The reward for this action instantiates the general formula of Section~\ref{sec:reward} against a fully invested SPY buy-and-hold counterfactual, using the vector form $G_t^{\text{paa}}$ of the execution-gap term (Section~\ref{sec:execgap}), since $\Delta w_t$ and $\Delta w_t^{\text{exec}}$ above are exactly the raw and realized weight deltas that formula compares.

Three reference accounts must be distinguished. The reward counterfactual is fully invested SPY buy-and-hold, paying its initial transaction cost and then retaining the position. A diagnostic comparison account retains the live portfolio's initial allocation without further trading. A second diagnostic benchmark starts at \(45\%\) SPY, \(20\%\) Gold, \(10\%\) EWJ, \(10\%\) EWG, and \(5\%\) each in Crude, EWQ, and EWT. Only the SPY account enters the excess-return reward; the other two provide comparisons rather than training targets.

\subsubsection{Network Architecture: Shared Attention with Private Heads}
\label{sec:paa-network}

The PAA is an \emph{encoder-only, identity-aware, cross-sectional Transformer actor--critic}. Encoder-only means that attention transforms the current asset and portfolio tokens without a decoder that generates an output sequence. Each decision requests one joint rebalancing vector, not a sequence of future trades, so contextual encoding followed by a fixed-size allocation head matches the control problem. Attention operates across assets and the portfolio at the current date; the PAA has no recurrent temporal state of its own.

Figure~\ref{fig:paa-arch} shows shared attention followed by separate actor and critic processing. The shared encoder has two layers, four attention heads, a 64-dimensional embedding, and 128-unit feed-forward blocks. Normalization precedes each residual block, with zero configured Transformer dropout and a Gaussian error linear unit (GELU), a smooth nonlinear activation. Residual connections add the transformed input to the original input, helping information propagate. One private encoder layer per output branch adapts the shared representation to policy and value estimation.

A scaled learned identity embedding distinguishes assets without allowing identity to dominate market features. This is an identity-aware policy, not an allocation rule over anonymous interchangeable tokens. Shared attention retains permutation-equivariant processing when token content and identity move together, but the complete policy also uses ordered portfolio fields and fixed asset-specific output positions. Mean pooling therefore does not establish permutation invariance or guarantee that arbitrarily reordering inputs reorders allocations correctly.

The actor's private layer processes the eleven encoded asset tokens and averages their representations before producing eleven risky-asset logits. The critic's private layer processes only the encoded portfolio token and estimates a scalar value. Both branches have already received portfolio-wide information through the shared encoder, so this routing does not prevent the actor from using portfolio context or the critic from using asset context. Each private layer receives gradients from its own objective, while both losses still influence the shared encoder.

\subsubsection{Actor-Critic Interaction}

The actor and critic outputs remain separate (Section~\ref{sec:actor-critic-theory}). The critic influences learning through advantage estimates and shared-encoder gradients, not through the executed allocation. A miscalibrated value estimate can therefore weaken the actor's training signal even though it never enters the trade directly. Initial weight scales account for layer width and depth to limit unstable signal propagation through the shared residual paths.
