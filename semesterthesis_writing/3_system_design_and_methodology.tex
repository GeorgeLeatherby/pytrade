\section{System Design and Methodology}
\label{sec:system-design}

PyTrade separates temporal signal generation from cross-asset allocation. Sections~\ref{sec:simulator}, \ref{sec:universe}, and~\ref{sec:friction} first establish the simulator, asset universe, and trading costs shared by both agents. Section~\ref{sec:overview} then formalizes their decision problems. Sections~\ref{sec:state} and~\ref{sec:reward} describe the features and reward methodology, before Sections~\ref{sec:saa} and~\ref{sec:paa} explain the agents and their signal-transfer interface.

\subsection{Simulator Design}
\label{sec:simulator}

Both, the SAA and the PAA agents interact with one custom trading simulator. A training episode begins by randomly selecting a historical interval and initializing cash and holdings. Each trading-day step supplies an observation, executes the requested action at the current closing price, applies trading costs, advances to the next date, and returns a reward and updated account information. Historical prices are exogenous, meaning that the agent changes its portfolio, but these changes do not change the market-price path. This is covered by the modeled impact costs explained later in section \ref{sec:friction}. To allow different agents to use the same simulator several execution modes are introduced, which differ in the portfolios they track, the action space allowed and the observations returned.

\subsubsection{Execution Modes and Data Infrastructure}

The simulator supports scalar single-asset position changes, vector-based portfolio-weight allocations, and two instruction-based execution modes in the form of lists for manual and rule-based tests. All modes share market data and the transaction-cost model. Separate shadow accounts allow the frozen SAA to trade hypothetical portfolios without observing the PAA's live trades. This combination extends the requirements of standard single-policy trading environments, such as those provided by FinRL \cite{liu2022finrldeepreinforcementlearning}.

Market features are computed once and reused across episodes. Account-dependent quantities are updated after each step. A rolling record retains prices, weights, returns, and risk statistics for observation and reward construction. Cash earns a daily return \(q_t\) derived from the Effective Federal Funds Rate (EFFR) \(f_t\), an annualized measure of overnight unsecured lending between financial institutions. The annual rate \(f_t\) in decimal units is divided by 252 and applied to cash after trading. Equation~\eqref{eq:cash-rate} defines the rate inputs separately from the offline market features. This is a modeled cash-return proxy, not a directly investable asset and is discussed in further detail in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\subsubsection{Purged Training, Validation, and Test Blocks}

The available history is divided into repeated training and validation blocks, followed by one final test block:
\begin{equation}
[\text{train}_0][\text{purge}][\text{val}_0]\,[\text{train}_1][\text{purge}][\text{val}_1]\,\cdots\,[\text{purge}][\text{test}].
\end{equation}
Each training--validation pair reserves approximately \(20\%\) of its usable dates for validation, separated from training by a 60-trading-day purge. The final test reserves exactly the last 252 market dates and also has a preceding purge. A purge is an interval excluded from both adjacent samples to reduce dependence through overlapping feature histories. During training an episode of 252 consecutive market dates is picked from one training block. This gives 251 daily transitions unless the episode terminates early. Blocks are sampled in proportion to their length, followed by a uniformly sampled admissible start date within the selected block. Overlapping episodes do not make every date equally likely to be used. Evaluation uses the fixed blocks described in Section~\ref{sec:oos-protocol}.

Rolling feature values use market observations up to their observation date. The fractional-differentiation order \(d^\ast\) is selected on pre-test history, including validation dates, rather than fitted separately within each training block (Section~\ref{sec:state-ffd}). The purge exceeds the dominant 36-day normalization window. Chained rolling calculations can have longer combined histories. Fractional differentiation can in theory use up to 120 observations before subsequent 36-day normalization. For the actual order \(d^\ast=0.85\), however, its normalized feature \(\phi_t\) uses only 50 trading dates and has no price-history overlap across the 60-day purge (Section~\ref{sec:state-ffd}). No additional purge is placed between a validation block and the following training block. Consequently, this is a purged historical block holdout, not strict walk-forward retraining in which every evaluated date follows all training dates.

The protocol follows the motivation for purged financial evaluation discussed by L\'opez de Prado \cite{LopezdePrado.2018}: serial dependence and overlapping histories can make shuffled validation misleading. Several historical validation blocks expose regime dependence, while the final test remains excluded from design and checkpoint selection. Full combinatorial purged cross-validation, which evaluates many combinations of held-out blocks, is not used because repeated complete policy training would exceed the computational budget.

\subsubsection{Hardware Placement and Batched Computation}

Environment execution runs on central processing units (CPUs), while neural-network calculations run on a graphics processing unit (GPU). CPUs suit branching account logic. A GPU processes large groups of similar matrix operations efficiently. With \(k\) parallel environments and \(N=11\) assets, the frozen SAA is evaluated over \(kN\) asset rows together, retaining separate recurrent states. Its standalone observations use the saved SAA normalization statistics. The augmented PAA observations are then normalized with separate PAA statistics. The PAA processes the \(k\) observations as another batch. Figure~\ref{fig:sim-classes} shows these separate normalization paths and the feedback loop from account execution to the next observation.

\subsection{Asset Universe}
\label{sec:universe}

The simulator trades eleven series: SPY for United States equities; the country ETFs EWG, EWH, EWJ, EWQ, EWS, EWT, EWU, and EWY for Germany, Hong Kong, Japan, France, Singapore, Taiwan, the United Kingdom, and South Korea; and Crude oil and Gold futures. A futures contract agrees to exchange an asset at a specified future date, but these series use simplified directly held asset accounting rather than explicit margin, expiry, and rollover (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). The country funds are dollar-quoted iShares products. Common dollar denomination avoids an additional currency-conversion step; it does not remove foreign-currency exposure embedded in their underlying holdings.

Daily OHLCV data originate from publicly available Investing.com historical datasets.\footnote{\url{https://www.investing.com/}} The aligned, feature-enriched dataset contains 6,344 market dates from 12 December 2000 to 6 August 2026. All agent variants use the same dataset and the training, validation, and test blocks in Table~\ref{tab:data-blocks}. Purge dates are excluded from the adjacent samples as described in Section~\ref{sec:simulator}.

\subsection{Trading Frictions}
\label{sec:friction}

Every executed trade pays commission, half-spread, and a size-dependent impact charge, plus an optional fixed fee. The commission rate \(c_{\text{comm}}\) represents a broker charge. The half-spread rate \(c_{\text{spread}}\) approximates the cost of crossing quoted bid and offer prices. Average daily dollar volume \(\mathrm{ADV}_j\) is the trailing mean of volume times closing price for asset \(j\), excluding the execution date and floored at one dollar. For traded notional \(n_j\), the monetary value of an order in asset \(j\), total transaction cost \(\mathrm{Cost}_t\) is
\begin{equation}
\text{Cost}_t = c_{\text{comm}}\sum_j n_j + c_{\text{spread}}\sum_j n_j
+ \lambda_{\text{impact}}\sum_j n_j\sqrt{\frac{n_j}{\text{ADV}_j}}
+ f_{\text{fixed}}\cdot\#\{\text{orders}\}.
\label{eq:trading-cost}
\end{equation}
The impact coefficient \(\lambda_{\text{impact}}\) controls the size-dependent charge. Its square-root dependence is sublinear per dollar, although total impact cost grows as \(n_j^{3/2}\). The fixed fee \(f_{\text{fixed}}\) is charged per executed order. The coefficients and liquidity window are fixed for each experiment. Setting the charges to zero permits a frictionless comparison, whereas the reported evaluations use configured costs. Together, simulator execution, the asset universe, and these frictions define the decision problem formalized next.

\subsection{System Overview}
\label{sec:overview}

The Single-Asset Agent (SAA) learns position changes for one asset at a time, while the Portfolio Allocator Agent (PAA) allocates capital across all eleven plus cash. The SAA is trained first and frozen. The PAA using its signals is called the hierarchical PAA. The hierarchical PAA receives the SAA's per-asset actions alongside market and account information. It can use, discount, or override these signals. This separates temporal representation from cross-sectional allocation without assuming that transfer must improve performance.

At each trading day, the observation \(o_t\) supplies market features and account information. The policy emits an action \(a_t\), interpreted as a position change or allocation request. The simulator executes it, advances to the next date, and returns reward \(R_{t+1}\). Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) updates network parameters from batches of these trajectories \cite{JohnSchulmanFilipWolskiPrafullaDhariwalAlecRadfordOlegKlimov.2017}. Episodes differ in their historical intervals and initial accounts, but replayed prices do not respond to the SAA's or hierarchical PAA's trades.

Splitting temporal modeling from cross-sectional allocation responds to two structures in the decision problem. The SAA uses a Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network to retain information across dates \cite{Hochreiter1997}. Cross-sectional allocation instead compares assets at the same date. Self-attention learns content-dependent weights for these comparisons \cite{vaswani2017}. The hierarchical PAA remains identity-aware and uses ordered portfolio fields and asset-specific output positions (Section~\ref{sec:paa}). PyTrade trains the SAA first and the hierarchical PAA second, so the hierarchical PAA can learn how much to rely on the frozen SAA's information.

We formalize both layers' decision problems with the same general tuple and instantiate it twice, with agent-specific state, action, and observation spaces, in Sections~\ref{sec:saa} and~\ref{sec:paa}. A Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP) represents decisions when the observation does not reveal the complete state \cite{SuttonBarto}. Here the POMDP \(\mathcal M\) is a tuple

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{M} = (\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, \mathcal{O}, \mathcal{P}, \mathcal{R}, \mathcal{Z}),
\end{equation}

The state space $\mathcal{S}$ contains simulator states, the action space $\mathcal{A}$ contains available actions, and the observation space $\mathcal{O}$ contains policy inputs. The transition rule $\mathcal{P}$ advances the simulator, the reward function $\mathcal{R}$ scores outcomes, and the observation map $\mathcal{Z}$ selects visible information. Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE) combines value-prediction errors across time to estimate how an action performed relative to expectation (Equation~\eqref{eq:gae}). The discount factor $\gamma=0.988$ reduces the weight of later rewards in the described configurations. The GAE decay parameter $\lambda=0.98$ controls reliance on longer trajectories. The finite episode horizon does not require discounting, but these settings provide a bias--variance trade-off during learning.


Partial observability arises because daily prices and engineered features omit market-relevant information, including news, fundamentals, and order-book conditions. The SAA's recurrent state \(h_t\) summarizes past observations as a response to this information gap \cite{hausknecht2015deep}. The hierarchical PAA has no recurrent state of its own, but receives historical context through engineered features, running account statistics, and frozen SAA signals. A memoryless policy does not make the observation fully Markovian.

\subsection{State Representation: A Shared Feature Engineering Pipeline}
\label{sec:state}

Raw price levels are generally non-stationary. A weakly \emph{stationary} process has a constant mean and variance, with covariance depending on the lag rather than calendar time \cite{Schmitt_2013}. Features whose typical scale changes between training and evaluation can weaken a learned decision rule. The enrichment pipeline therefore transforms opening price \(O_t\), high price \(H_t\), low price \(L_t\), closing price \(C_t\), and volume \(Q_t\), collectively OHLCV. Below, price \(P_t=C_t\) denotes the close. Market features are computed offline and reused. Cash, weights, costs, and other action-dependent quantities are updated each step.

Two transformations recur. The log return \(r_t^{(h)}\) over horizon \(h\), defined in Equation~\eqref{eq:feature-returns}, represents a proportional rather than dollar change and adds across time. Additivity still records the net loss from a \(+10\%\) move followed by \(-10\%\). Rolling \(z\)-score standardization expresses a series value \(x_t\) relative to its trailing mean \(\mu_w(x)_t\) and standard deviation \(\sigma_w(x)_t\). We write this operation as
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{z}_{w}(x)_t = \frac{x_t-\mu_w(x)_t}{\sigma_w(x)_t+\epsilon}.
\label{eq:feature-zscore}
\end{equation}
The stabilizer \(\epsilon=10^{-8}\) prevents division by zero. The window length \(w\) counts trading dates and includes the current date. Market-feature standard deviations \(\sigma_w\) use the sample estimate. Where partial windows are allowed, standardization starts after five valid observations. The notation \(\sigma_w^{(0)}\) instead denotes a population estimate. Most market-feature windows use \(w=36\). The superscript \((0,\mathrm{cal})\) on \(\operatorname z\) will identify population standardization on calendar days for EFFR. The hyperbolic tangent \(\tanh(x)\) bounds a transformed value between \(-1\) and \(1\). Clipping \(\operatorname{clip}(x,a,b)\) caps a value at lower bound \(a\) and upper bound \(b\). Feature selection is fixed separately for the SAA and the hierarchical PAA's asset and portfolio tokens (Sections~\ref{sec:saa} and~\ref{sec:paa}).

\subsubsection{Multi-Horizon Return, Volume, and Volatility Features}
\label{sec:state-returns}

The full-feature configuration selects return horizons \(h\in\{1,5,21\}\), representing daily, weekly, and approximately monthly information. The symbol \(N=11\) remains reserved for the asset count. For each selected horizon \(h\), asset log return \(r_t^{(h)}\), SPY log return \(r_t^{(h),\mathrm{SPY}}\), and relative log return \(\tilde r_t^{(h)}\) are

\begin{equation}
r_t^{(h)}=\log\frac{P_t}{P_{t-h}},\qquad
r_t^{(h),\mathrm{SPY}}=\log\frac{P_t^{\mathrm{SPY}}}{P_{t-h}^{\mathrm{SPY}}},\qquad
\tilde r_t^{(h)}=r_t^{(h)}-r_t^{(h),\mathrm{SPY}}.
\label{eq:feature-returns}
\end{equation}

The raw log return \(r_t^{(h)}\) supplies directional information. The standardized relative-return feature \(z_{\mathrm{rel},t}^{(h)}\) measures unusual movement against SPY:

\begin{equation}
z_{\mathrm{rel},t}^{(h)}=\operatorname{clip}\big(\operatorname z_{36}(\tilde r^{(h)})_t,-4,4\big).
\label{eq:feature-relative-returns}
\end{equation}

The bounds limit the relative-return feature \(z_{\mathrm{rel},t}^{(h)}\) to \([-4,4]\), while the raw log return \(r_t^{(h)}\) remains available.

A volatility-breakout feature \(z_{\mathrm{vol},t}^{(h)}\) measures unusual changes in short-term relative-return variability. Its intermediate log volatility ratio \(u_t^{(h)}\) compares five-day and 36-day standard deviations:

\begin{equation}
u_t^{(h)}=\log\frac{\sigma_5(\tilde r^{(h)})_t+\epsilon}{\sigma_{36}(\tilde r^{(h)})_t+\epsilon},\qquad
z_{\mathrm{vol},t}^{(h)}=\operatorname{clip}\big(\operatorname z_{36}(u^{(h)})_t,-4,4\big).
\label{eq:feature-vol-breakout}
\end{equation}

The volatility ratio \(u_t^{(h)}\) is standardized separately for each asset and horizon \(h\). SPY's relative log return \(\tilde r_t^{(h)}\), volatility-breakout feature \(z_{\mathrm{vol},t}^{(h)}\), and standardized relative return \(z_{\mathrm{rel},t}^{(h)}\) are zero by construction.

Volume intensity \(I_t^{(h)}\) compares recent mean volume with its 36-day baseline:
\begin{equation}
I_t^{(h)}=\log\frac{\max(\mu_h(Q)_t,\epsilon)}{\mu_{36}(Q)_t+\epsilon}.
\label{eq:feature-volume}
\end{equation}
The market-relative volume feature \(v_t^{(h)}\) compares volume intensity \(I_t^{(h)}\) with SPY's same-horizon intensity \(I_t^{(h),\mathrm{SPY}}\):
\begin{equation}
v_t^{(h)}=\tanh\!\big(\operatorname z_{36}(I^{(h)}-I^{(h),\mathrm{SPY}})_t\big).
\label{eq:feature-relative-volume}
\end{equation}
SPY's relative volume feature \(v_t^{(h)}\) is zero. Each selected horizon \(h\) uses a different recent volume average.

\subsubsection{Intraday Shape and Classical Technical Indicators}

A second group describes the daily price bar. Log range \(\ell_t^{\mathrm{range}}\) measures intraday movement, closing strength \(c_t^{\mathrm{bar}}\) locates the close within that range, overnight gap \(g_t^{\mathrm{open}}\) compares the open with the preceding close, and upper-wick fraction \(u_t^{\mathrm{wick}}\) measures the range above both open and close:

\begin{equation}
\ell_t^{\mathrm{range}}=\log\frac{H_t}{L_t},\quad
c_t^{\mathrm{bar}}=\frac{C_t-L_t}{H_t-L_t},\quad
g_t^{\mathrm{open}}=\log\frac{O_t}{C_{t-1}},\quad
u_t^{\mathrm{wick}}=\frac{H_t-\max(O_t,C_t)}{H_t-L_t}.
\label{eq:feature-bars-raw}
\end{equation}

Their four model inputs \(\psi_t^{\mathrm{range}},\psi_t^{\mathrm{close}},\psi_t^{\mathrm{gap}},\psi_t^{\mathrm{wick}}\) are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\psi_t^{\mathrm{range}}&=\tanh\!\big(\operatorname z_{36}(\ell^{\mathrm{range}})_t\big),
&\psi_t^{\mathrm{close}}&=2c_t^{\mathrm{bar}}-1,\\
\psi_t^{\mathrm{gap}}&=\tanh\!\big(\operatorname z_{36}(g^{\mathrm{open}})_t\big),
&\psi_t^{\mathrm{wick}}&=2u_t^{\mathrm{wick}}-1.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:feature-bars}
\end{equation}
Standardization and rescaling make these bar features dimensionless and bounded.

Three classical technical indicators provide additional summaries of momentum and variability. Their transformed inputs are also dimensionless.

The \emph{Relative Strength Index} \(\mathrm{RSI}_{14,t}\) compares average gains with average losses over 14 trading days. For price change \(\Delta P_t=P_t-P_{t-1}\) and positive part \(x^+=\max(x,0)\), relative strength \(\mathrm{RS}_t\) and the transformed RSI feature \(\psi_t^{\mathrm{RSI}}\) are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{RS}_t&=\frac{\mu_{14}((\Delta P)^+)_t}{\mu_{14}((-\Delta P)^+)_t+\epsilon},
&\mathrm{RSI}_{14,t}&=100-\frac{100}{1+\mathrm{RS}_t},\\
\psi_t^{\mathrm{RSI}}&=\mu_3\!\left(\frac{\mathrm{RSI}_{14}-50}{50}\right)_t.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:feature-rsi}
\end{equation}
Raw RSI values \(\mathrm{RSI}_{14,t}>70\) or \(<30\) are conventionally called overbought or oversold, not guarantees of reversal.

A \emph{Bollinger Band} surrounds mean price \(\mu_{36}(P)_t\) with an envelope at two standard deviations \(\sigma_{36}(P)_t\). Relative band width \(\mathrm{BW}_t\) measures the envelope's width relative to price. The transformed band-width feature \(\psi_t^{\mathrm{BB}}\) is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{BW}_t=\frac{4\sigma_{36}(P)_t}{\mu_{36}(P)_t+\epsilon},\qquad
\psi_t^{\mathrm{BB}}=\operatorname{clip}\big(\operatorname z_{36}(\mathrm{BW})_t,-3,3\big).
\label{eq:feature-bb}
\end{equation}
Larger raw band width \(\mathrm{BW}_t\) indicates greater recent relative price variability.

The \emph{Average True Range} \(\mathrm{ATR}_t\) measures variability including opening gaps. True range \(\mathrm{TR}_t\) accounts for movement from the previous close. The transformed ATR feature \(\psi_t^{\mathrm{ATR}}\) is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{TR}_t&=\max(H_t-L_t,|H_t-C_{t-1}|,|L_t-C_{t-1}|),\\
\mathrm{ATR}_t&=\mu_{36}(\mathrm{TR})_t,\qquad
\psi_t^{\mathrm{ATR}}=\operatorname{clip}\big(\operatorname z_{36}(\mathrm{ATR})_t,-3,3\big).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:feature-atr}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Cross-Asset Features}

Three features make each asset's representation depend explicitly on SPY or the full universe. They provide cross-asset context to the hierarchical PAA before attention weights are learned (Section~\ref{sec:paa}).

Pearson correlation \(\varrho_t\) measures linear co-movement between daily asset log return \(r_t^{(1)}\) and SPY log return \(r_t^{(1),\mathrm{SPY}}\) over 21 dates:
\begin{equation}
\varrho_t=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}_{21}(r^{(1)},r^{(1),\mathrm{SPY}})_t}
{\sigma_{21}(r^{(1)})_t\,\sigma_{21}(r^{(1),\mathrm{SPY}})_t}.
\label{eq:feature-correlation}
\end{equation}
The correlation coefficient \(\varrho_t\) lies in \([-1,1]\). Its sampling distribution becomes skewed near the bounds. The Fisher-transformed correlation \(F_t\) reduces this boundary compression:

\begin{equation}
\tilde\varrho_t=\operatorname{clip}(\varrho_t,-0.99,0.99),\qquad
F_t=\frac12\log\frac{1+\tilde\varrho_t}{1-\tilde\varrho_t}.
\label{eq:feature-fisher}
\end{equation}

The Fisher transform \(F_t\) approximately stabilizes sampling variance. Clipped correlation \(\tilde\varrho_t\) keeps it finite. This transform is not the standardization operator \(\operatorname z_w\) in Equation~\eqref{eq:feature-zscore}. The model receives \(F_t\) without additional rolling standardization or \(\tanh\).

Rolling beta \(\beta_t\) measures sensitivity to SPY's returns through the covariance-to-market-variance ratio. The standardized beta feature \(\beta_t^z\) is
\begin{equation}
\beta_t=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}_{36}(r^{(1)},r^{(1),\mathrm{SPY}})_t}
{\sigma_{36}(r^{(1),\mathrm{SPY}})_t^2+\epsilon},\qquad
\beta_t^z=\operatorname{clip}\big(\operatorname z_{36}(\beta)_t,-3,3\big).
\label{eq:feature-beta}
\end{equation}
Raw beta \(\beta_t>1\) indicates greater historical sensitivity than SPY, not a threshold on standardized beta \(\beta_t^z\).

Cross-sectional dispersion \(D_t\) measures how differently the eleven assets move on the same date. Its standardized feature \(D_t^z\) is
\begin{equation}
D_t=\log\!\left(\operatorname{sd}_{i=1,\dots,N}(r_{i,t}^{(1)})+\epsilon\right),\qquad
D_t^z=\operatorname z_{36}(D)_t.
\label{eq:feature-dispersion}
\end{equation}
The cross-sectional standard deviation \(\operatorname{sd}\) uses the sample estimate across assets. Larger dispersion \(D_t^z\) indicates unusual cross-sectional variation, not necessarily an exploitable opportunity.

\subsubsection{Memory-Preserving Stationary Features via Fractional Differentiation}
\label{sec:state-ffd}

Ordinary differencing removes price-level information and can weaken slow-moving trend context. Fractional differentiation instead combines log prices with gradually decaying weights governed by a non-integer order \(d\), retaining some historical contribution while reducing non-stationarity \cite{LopezdePrado.2018}. Smaller orders preserve more memory but may leave stronger non-stationarity. Figure~\ref{fig:ffd-pipeline} summarizes the fixed-width approximation and its empirical checks.

The search tests orders from \(0.45\) to \(0.85\) in increments of \(0.05\), using SPY, Gold, and Crude as anchor assets. Fitting explicitly excludes the final 252 market dates, to ensure out-of-sample testing. It uses the preceding history, including validation dates. To asses the stationarity of features the Augmented Dickey--Fuller (ADF) test is used. The ADF test assesses the null hypothesis of a unit root, a persistent random-walk component. A result of \(p<0.05\) rejects that null at the chosen significance level. It is evidence against this form of non-stationarity, not proof of stationarity under every definition \cite{LopezdePrado.2018}. The largest of the anchors' smallest passing orders is applied across assets so their transformation is comparable.

The generated dataset uses \(d^\ast=0.85\), required to meet the stated ADF acceptance criterion during pre-test calibration. For this order, the causal fixed-width filter is
\begin{equation}
w_0=1,\qquad w_k=-w_{k-1}\frac{d^\ast-k+1}{k},\qquad
\widetilde X_t=\sum_{k=0}^{L-1}w_k\log P_{t-k},\qquad L\leq120.
\label{eq:feature-ffd-filter}
\end{equation}
Filter coefficients \(w_k\) stop before the first magnitude below cutoff \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{ffd}}=10^{-3}\), with filter length \(L\leq120\). The newest log price \(\log P_t\) receives coefficient \(w_0=1\). Stored coefficients run from oldest to newest and are applied as a rolling dot product in that order. The same finite filter is applied throughout the dataset. The final fractional-price feature \(\phi_t\) is
\begin{equation}
\phi_t=\tanh\!\big(\operatorname z_{36}(\widetilde X)_t\big).
\label{eq:feature-ffd}
\end{equation}
The final ADF checks use only pre-test dates. Every asset must reject the unit-root null before generation is accepted. The saved metadata records the selected order \(d^\ast\), coefficients \(w_k\), feature columns, and fit and test boundaries.

At \(d^\ast=0.85\), the untruncated coefficient at lag 60 is approximately \(w_{60}=-7.11\times10^{-5}\), only \(0.0071\%\) of the current-price coefficient. The implemented cutoff is more restrictive. It retains \(L=15\) coefficients, covering lags zero through fourteen. Including the 36-day normalization, the final feature depends on at most \(15+36-1=50\) trading dates, with the oldest price at lag 49. With the fitted order held fixed, a price 60 trading days old therefore has zero influence apart from numerical rounding. The 60-day purge thus prevents overlapping price history for this feature. Excluding the test period from order selection and ADF acceptance additionally prevents test-period fitting leakage. This does not imply training-only preprocessing for validation, whose dates participate in calibration.

\subsubsection{Calendar Encoding}

A calendar vector \(\mathbf g_t\in\mathbb R^6\) encodes weekday, relative calendar-day position within the month, and month of year. For cycle position \(k_t\) and cycle length \(K\), sine and cosine inputs \(x_t^{\sin}\) and \(x_t^{\cos}\) are

\begin{equation}
x^{\sin}_t = \sin\!\Big(\tfrac{2\pi\, k_t}{K}\Big), \qquad x^{\cos}_t = \cos\!\Big(\tfrac{2\pi\, k_t}{K}\Big),öseries \texttt{DFF} supplies annual percentage rate \(\mathrm{DFF}_t\), converted to annual decimal rate \(f_t\). The query includes 120 calendar days before the first required market date. Missing calendar dates are forward-filled. Any remaining leading gaps use the first available value. Daily cash return \(q_t\), standardized cash-rate input \(z_t^{\mathrm{rf}}\), and optional rate-level input \(\ell_t^{\mathrm{rf}}\) are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
f_t&=\frac{\mathrm{DFF}_t}{100},&q_t&=\frac{f_t}{252},\\
z_t^{\mathrm{rf}}&=\operatorname{clip}\big(\operatorname z_{60}^{(0,\mathrm{cal})}(f)_t,-3,3\big),
&\ell_t^{\mathrm{rf}}&=\frac{f_t}{0.1}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:cash-rate}
\end{equation}
The calendar-based rate input \(z_t^{\mathrm{rf}}\) uses 60 complete calendar observations and a population standard deviation. The rate fields are then aligned to market dates. They are appended to account observations rather than counted among the 26 market indicators. The selected asset's excess log return over cash \(\alpha_t^{i,\mathrm{rf}}\) is also computed directly from cached closes:
\begin{equation}
\alpha_t^{i,\mathrm{rf}}=\log\frac{P_t^{(i)}}{P_{t-1}^{(i)}}-\log(1+q_t).
\label{eq:asset-excess-cash}
\end{equation}
The SAA context in Equation~\eqref{eq:saa-context} receives standardized cash rate \(z_t^{\mathrm{rf}}\) and asset excess return \(\alpha_t^{i,\mathrm{rf}}\). The hierarchical PAA's portfolio diagnostics in Equation~\eqref{eq:paa-diagnostics} receive standardized cash rate \(z_t^{\mathrm{rf}}\), daily cash return \(q_t\), and portfolio excess return over cash \(\alpha_t^{\mathrm{pf,rf}}\). These account-level paths are distinct from selecting columns for the market-feature vector.

Feature selection is fixed separately for the SAA, hierarchical PAA asset tokens, and hierarchical PAA portfolio token in each experiment. This separates changes in available information from changes in neural architecture and supports the feature ablation reported in Chapter~4.

\subsection{A Shared Reward Design Methodology}
\label{sec:reward}

Both layers use the same reward methodology for two notions of ``portfolio'': the SAA's cash-plus-one-asset account (Section~\ref{sec:saa}) and the hierarchical PAA's live multi-asset account (Section~\ref{sec:paa}). The reward \(R_t\) scores the transition ending at date \(t\), following action \(a_{t-1}\). It is \emph{dense}, meaning that it is supplied at every daily transition rather than only at episode termination. This gives PPO intermediate targets for its value estimates \(v_t\) and advantage estimates \(\hat A_t\) \cite{SuttonBarto,JohnSchulmanFilipWolskiPrafullaDhariwalAlecRadfordOlegKlimov.2017}.

\paragraph{Multi-Objective Reward Composition.}

Raw reward \(R_t^{\mathrm{raw}}\) combines excess log return \(\Delta\ell_t\), differential Sortino \(\Delta S_t\), incremental drawdown \(\Delta\mathrm{DD}_t^+\), and persistent drawdown \(\mathrm{DD}_t^{\mathrm{level}}\). The SAA additionally includes execution gap \(G_{t-1}\) from the preceding action. These terms are defined below. For the described configurations, optional realized-profit exit bonuses have coefficient zero:

\begin{equation}
R_t^{\mathrm{raw}}=s_\alpha\Delta\ell_t+s_S\Delta S_t
-\lambda_{dd}\Delta\mathrm{DD}_t^+
-\lambda_{dd}^{\mathrm{lvl}}\mathrm{DD}_t^{\mathrm{level}}
-\lambda_{\mathrm{exec}}G_{t-1}.
\label{eq:reward-composition}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
R_t = \tanh\!\Big(\frac{R_t^{\text{raw}}}{\tau}\Big) \cdot \sigma_R.
\label{eq:reward-squash}
\end{equation}

The return scale \(s_\alpha\), Sortino scale \(s_S\), drawdown coefficients \(\lambda_{dd}\) and \(\lambda_{dd}^{\mathrm{lvl}}\), and execution-gap coefficient \(\lambda_{\mathrm{exec}}\) control the contributions in Equation~\eqref{eq:reward-composition}. The saturation divisor \(\tau\) and output scale \(\sigma_R\) bound environment reward \(R_t\) to \((-\sigma_R,\sigma_R)\) in Equation~\eqref{eq:reward-squash}. Training subsequently applies running reward normalization, so this bound does not describe the normalized learning signal. Evaluation reports unnormalized rewards \(R_t\). The SAA and hierarchical PAA use different passive return counterfactuals. Persistent drawdown coefficient \(\lambda_{dd}^{\mathrm{lvl}}\) is zero in the described configurations. The hierarchical PAA records execution gap \(G_t^{\mathrm{paa}}\) but does not subtract it from reward, so its effective coefficient \(\lambda_{\mathrm{exec}}\) is zero.

\paragraph{Excess Return over a Passive Counterfactual.}
\label{sec:excess-return}

Excess return compares an active account with a passive counterfactual, meaning a reference account that does not make new trading decisions. Portfolio value \(V_t\) denotes the SAA's account or the hierarchical PAA's live account. Counterfactual value \(B_t\) defines excess log return \(\Delta\ell_t\):

\begin{equation}
\Delta\ell_t = \big(\log V_t - \log V_{t-1}\big) - \big(\log B_t - \log B_{t-1}\big).
\label{eq:reward-excess}
\end{equation}

Excess log return \(\Delta\ell_t\) measures relative performance rather than raw market exposure. For initial capital \(c_0\) and selected asset price \(p_t\), the SAA uses counterfactual value \(B_t=c_0p_t/p_0\) without transaction costs. Its separately tracked, transaction-cost-paying same-asset buy-and-hold account is diagnostic only. The hierarchical PAA instead uses its transaction-cost-paying SPY buy-and-hold value \(B_t\). Section~\ref{sec:paa-action} distinguishes this reference from the hierarchical PAA's other diagnostic portfolios.

\paragraph{Risk-Adjusted Trend: The Differential Sortino Ratio.}

The Sortino ratio \(S_t\) measures mean return per unit of downside deviation, so positive returns do not increase its risk denominator. The implementation uses a smoothed daily estimator rather than a full-episode statistic. Net account return \(r_t^{\mathrm{pf}}\) is
\begin{equation}
r_t^{\mathrm{pf}}=\frac{V_t}{V_{t-1}}-1.
\label{eq:account-return}
\end{equation}
Net account return \(r_t^{\mathrm{pf}}\) includes charged transaction costs and positive cash carry. An exponential moving average (EMA) blends each new observation into a running estimate with update weight \(\eta\), discounting older observations geometrically. EMA mean return \(\bar r_t\) and downside second moment \(\bar v_t^-\) follow

\begin{equation}
\bar r_t=\bar r_{t-1}+\eta(r_t^{\mathrm{pf}}-\bar r_{t-1}),\qquad
\bar v_t^-=\bar v_{t-1}^-+\eta\big(\min(r_t^{\mathrm{pf}},0)^2-\bar v_{t-1}^-\big).
\label{eq:sortino-ema}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
d_t^-=\sqrt{\max(\bar v_t^-,v_{\min})},\qquad
S_t=\frac{\bar r_t}{d_t^-},\qquad
\Delta S_t=
\begin{cases}
0,&t<T_{\mathrm{warm}},\\
S_t-S_{t-1},&t\geq T_{\mathrm{warm}}.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:sortino}
\end{equation}

Downside deviation \(d_t^-\) is the square root of an exponentially weighted squared shortfall below a zero-return target. It is not a standard deviation conditional on negative periods. Variance floor \(v_{\min}=0.000025\) gives a minimum downside deviation \(d_t^-=0.005\). The Sortino estimate \(S_t\) is neither annualized nor adjusted by subtracting the cash rate \(q_t\). Here ``differential'' means the finite change \(\Delta S_t\), not an analytical derivative. Warm-up threshold \(T_{\mathrm{warm}}=8\) suppresses the reward contribution before transition eight while the EMA estimates are updated. This supplies daily risk-adjusted feedback without waiting for episode termination.

\paragraph{Drawdown Penalties.}
\label{sec:drawdown}

A \emph{drawdown} is a percentage decline from an earlier portfolio-value peak. Rolling maximum drawdown \(M_t\) is the largest such decline within interval \(I_t=\{\max(0,t-W_t),\dots,t\}\), using risk-window length \(W_t\):
\begin{equation}
M_t=\operatorname{MDD}_{W_t}(V)_t
=\max_{u\in I_t}\left(1-\frac{V_u}{\max_{v\in I_t,\,v\leq u}V_v}\right),\qquad
\Delta\text{DD}_t^{+}=\max(0,M_t-M_{t-1}).
\label{eq:rolling-drawdown}
\end{equation}
Risk-window length \(W_t\) starts at two steps and grows to 63 steps for the 252-date training episodes. Current account value \(V_t\) is recorded before these calculations. Incremental drawdown \(\Delta\mathrm{DD}_t^+\) charges increases in rolling maximum drawdown \(M_t\), not every increase in the current distance from a peak. The optional persistent drawdown \(\mathrm{DD}_t^{\mathrm{level}}\) instead uses the whole-episode peak:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{DD}_t^{\mathrm{level}}=\max\!\left(0,1-\frac{V_t}{\max_{0\leq u\leq t}V_u}\right).
\label{eq:drawdown-level}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{The Execution Gap.}
\label{sec:execgap}

The SAA execution gap \(G_t^{\mathrm{saa}}\) measures the distance between scaled action request \(a_t\) and signed notional actually filled. A deadband exempts small gaps. Requests beyond available cash or inventory can still incur a penalty. The hierarchical PAA records a related gap diagnostic \(G_t^{\mathrm{paa}}\) from the soft-execution mechanics of Equation~\eqref{eq:paa-soft-execution}:

\begin{equation}
G_t^{\text{paa}} = \sum_{j=1}^{N} \max\big(0,\, \lvert \Delta w_t^{(j)} - \Delta w_t^{\text{soft},(j)} \rvert - \delta\big).
\label{eq:execution-gap-paa}
\end{equation}

Cash is excluded to avoid double counting. Soft weight change \(\Delta w_t^{\mathrm{soft},(j)}\) precedes weight renormalization and cash or inventory constraints, so gap diagnostic \(G_t^{\mathrm{paa}}\) is not a gap to final fills. It is not subtracted from the hierarchical PAA reward \(R_t\). For the SAA, executed action \(a_t^{\mathrm{exec}}=\Delta x_t^{\mathrm{filled}}p_t/V_t\) values signed filled shares \(\Delta x_t^{\mathrm{filled}}\) at execution price \(p_t\). The execution gap \(G_t^{\mathrm{saa}}\) is

\begin{equation}
G_t^{\text{saa}} = \max\big(0,\, \lvert a_t - a_t^{\text{exec}} \rvert - g_0\big),
\label{eq:execution-gap-saa}
\end{equation}

Here \(g_0=(n_{\min}+b_{\text{usd}})/V_t\), where \(n_{\min}\) is the larger of the execution minimum and SAA trade-value floor, and \(b_{\text{usd}}\) is a monetary buffer. The denominator is pre-trade account value. Subsequent market-price movement is not an executed action. Configured multipliers distinguish positive from non-positive signed gaps \(a_t-a_t^{\text{exec}}\).


\subsection{Layer 1: The Single-Asset Agent}
\label{sec:saa}

The SAA learns temporal trading decisions. Each training episode randomly assigns one asset, and the SAA decides whether to increase, reduce, or retain its position each step. A two-layer LSTM carries recurrent state \(h_t\) across trading days \cite{Hochreiter1997}. Market-relative features provide context, but the SAA cannot move its capital to another instrument within the episode.

\subsubsection{State, Observation, and Action Space}

At each step $t$ the SAA can hold the one asset selected for its episode, alongside cash. Its holdings may be zero. The relevant account-state slice is

\begin{equation}
s_t^{\text{saa}} = \big(p_t,\, c_t,\, x_t\big),
\label{eq:saa-account-state}
\end{equation}

the asset's close price $p_t$, the cash balance $c_t$, and the held share count $x_t$. This is not the complete simulator state. The date, selected asset, market panel, account history, previous actions, and running risk estimates also determine observations, rewards, and termination. Cash and holdings are endogenous, meaning that trades change them. Historical market prices are exogenous and evolve independently of those trades. Separating account and market state follows standard trading-environment descriptions such as FinRL \cite{liu2022finrldeepreinforcementlearning}.

The SAA observation \(o_t^{\mathrm{saa}}\) is constructed from the full simulator state as three concatenated blocks:


\begin{equation}
o_t^{\text{saa}} = \big[\, \mathbf{f}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{32},\ \ \mathbf{m}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{7},\ \ \mathbf{e}_i \in \{0,1\}^{11} \,\big]\in\mathbb{R}^{50},
\label{eq:saa-observation}
\end{equation}

Feature vector \(\mathbf f_t\) contains the 26 market indicators defined in Section~\ref{sec:state} and calendar vector \(\mathbf g_t\) from Equation~\eqref{eq:feature-calendar}. Asset identity \(\mathbf e_i\) is a one-hot vector with one entry equal to one for selected asset \(i\). Context vector \(\mathbf m_t\) contains account and additional market inputs. Feature flags select columns but do not specify their order. The cache preserves the supplied CSV column order. With the additional SPY context enabled, context vector \(\mathbf m_t\) is

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{m}_t = \Big(\log(1+\tfrac{c_t}{c_0}),\ \log(1+\tfrac{x_t p_t}{c_0}),\
r_t^{\text{sub}},\ a_{t-1},\ z_t^{\text{rf}},\ \alpha_t^{i,\text{rf}},\ r_t^{(1),\text{SPY}}\Big).
\label{eq:saa-context}
\end{equation}

The cash encoding \(\log(1+c_t/c_0)\) and holding encoding \(\log(1+x_tp_t/c_0)\) use initial capital \(c_0\) as reference. An empty balance encodes as zero and a balance equal to initial capital \(c_0\) as \(\log2\). SAA account return \(r_t^{\mathrm{sub}}\) is net return from Equation~\eqref{eq:account-return} for the SAA account. Previous scaled action request \(a_{t-1}\) records the last request, not the realized fill. Both are zero at reset. Standardized EFFR \(z_t^{\mathrm{rf}}\) follows Equation~\eqref{eq:cash-rate}. Asset excess return \(\alpha_t^{i,\mathrm{rf}}\) follows Equation~\eqref{eq:asset-excess-cash}, not an account-return calculation. SPY log return \(r_t^{(1),\mathrm{SPY}}\) follows Equation~\eqref{eq:feature-returns}.

Standardized EFFR \(z_t^{\mathrm{rf}}\) is controlled by \texttt{z\_score\_60\_effr}, enabled by default. Optional rate level \(\ell_t^{\mathrm{rf}}\) from Equation~\eqref{eq:cash-rate} is controlled by \texttt{effr\_level\_active}. When enabled, it is inserted after asset excess return \(\alpha_t^{i,\mathrm{rf}}\) and before SPY log return \(r_t^{(1),\mathrm{SPY}}\). The latter is controlled by \texttt{include\_spy\_log\_close\_return\_1d}. The described configuration enables standardized EFFR \(z_t^{\mathrm{rf}}\) and SPY log return \(r_t^{(1),\mathrm{SPY}}\), but disables rate level \(\ell_t^{\mathrm{rf}}\). This gives context dimension \(\dim\mathbf m_t=7\) and observation dimension \(\dim o_t^{\mathrm{saa}}=50\). Enabling rate level \(\ell_t^{\mathrm{rf}}\) adds one entry. Asset identity \(\mathbf e_i\) always remains the final eleven entries. Running observation normalization is applied after concatenation and is distinct from the rate standardization in Equation~\eqref{eq:cash-rate}.

The policy action \(\hat a_t\in\mathcal A_{\mathrm{saa}}=[-1,1]\) is a scalar \emph{target position change}, not a target weight. Action scale \(\kappa_t\) gives the scaled request \(a_t\) and requested signed trade notional \(n_t^{\mathrm{req}}\):
\begin{equation}
a_t=\operatorname{clip}(\kappa_t\hat a_t,-1,1),\qquad
n_t^{\mathrm{req}}=a_tV_t,\qquad V_t=c_t+x_tp_t.
\label{eq:saa-action}
\end{equation}
Current pre-trade account value \(V_t\), not initial capital \(c_0\), determines trade size. Thus policy action \(\hat a_t=1\) requests a buy of \(\kappa_tV_t\) dollars, while \(\hat a_t=-0.5\) requests a sale of \(0.5\kappa_tV_t\). Available cash, inventory, costs, and trade thresholds constrain fills. The three-phase schedule permits a warm-up, linear ramp, and terminal action scale \(\kappa_t\). Equal endpoints keep action scale \(\kappa_t\) constant, as in the described configuration. Reward \(R_t\) uses the selected asset's price log return as counterfactual in Equation~\eqref{eq:reward-excess}.

\subsubsection{Network Architecture}

Asset identity \(\mathbf e_i\) is mapped to a learned embedding \(\mathrm{emb}(i)\in\mathbb R^6\), a trainable numeric representation. Feature vector \(\mathbf f_t\), context vector \(\mathbf m_t\), and embedding \(\mathrm{emb}(i)\) enter a multilayer perceptron (MLP), a feed-forward network of dense layers. Layer normalization rescales intermediate features. A sigmoid linear unit (SiLU) supplies a smooth nonlinear activation. Dropout can randomly suppress intermediate units during training, but its configured probability is zero here \cite{Goodfellow-et-al-2016}. Figure~\ref{fig:saa-arch} shows how the identity path joins the continuous inputs before the separate actor and critic recurrences.

\paragraph{Actor and Critic Roles.}
\label{sec:actor-critic-theory}
Critic value estimate \(v_t\) provides a baseline for judging whether an action performed better than expected \cite{SuttonBarto}. It does not supply part of executed action \(a_t\). Actor and critic can influence shared representations during training, but only the actor's action distribution \(\pi_\theta\) is used for trading.

%\subsubsection{Why Actor and Critic Outputs Are Never Recombined}
%\label{sec:actor-critic-theory}

PPO, and actor-critic policy-gradient methods generally, do not follow this pattern, and the reason is more than convention.

%The two heads solve two different regression problems against two different targets. The actor parameterizes a probability distribution $\pi_\theta(a_t \vert s_t)$ over actions; PPO's clipped surrogate objective is defined purely through the probability ratio $\pi_\theta(a_t\vert s_t)/\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t\vert s_t)$ and requires that ratio to depend only on the current policy parameters. The critic regresses $V(s_t)$ toward an empirical return-to-go target for one purpose only: producing a lower-variance advantage estimate $\hat{A}_t = \hat{R}_t - V(s_t)$ for that same surrogate. $V(s_t)$ is a training-time variance-reduction baseline, not a candidate component of the emitted action; folding it into the action would make the executed trade depend on how well the value regression happens to have converged at that point in training, an entirely different and typically noisier optimization problem than the policy's own.

%Concretely, for the SAA, $V(s_t)$ is an unconstrained real number approximating a discounted sum of tanh-squashed rewards, with no reason to lie in $[-1,1]$; adding it to the actor's mean action would either require an ad hoc renormalization or would routinely push the executed target-position-change outside its valid range, injecting the critic's regression noise directly into a real trade and its transaction cost. For the PAA (Section~\ref{sec:paa-network}), the argument is sharper and purely structural rather than just a matter of numerical hygiene: the critic there produces a single scalar per state, and adding that same scalar to every one of the $N$ asset logits before the softmax of Section~\ref{sec:paa-action} would be mathematically inert, since softmax is shift-invariant, exactly the property used to justify fixing the cash logit at zero (which will be explained later). Recombining the two heads for the PAA would therefore not just be theoretically questionable; it would be a literal no-op wrapped around extra computation.

%Keeping the two heads' outputs disjoint costs nothing and buys a clean separation of concerns. The actor can be evaluated with the critic discarded entirely, exactly what happens for every frozen SAA copy in Section~\ref{sec:info-flow}, which reads only the actor's mean action and never instantiates a value estimate at inference time. The critic, symmetrically, can be reweighted (via \texttt{vf\_coef}) or even pretrained on offline Monte-Carlo targets without ever perturbing the executed action. This separation does not mean the two heads are fully independent, however; Section~\ref{sec:paa-network}'s discussion of actor-critic interaction details the two indirect channels through which they still shape one another's training.

\subsubsection{Recurrent Memory and Advantage Estimation}

Advantage estimate \(\hat A_t=\hat R_t-v_t\) compares empirical return-to-go target \(\hat R_t\), the estimated sum of future discounted rewards, with critic value estimate \(v_t\). A positive advantage estimate \(\hat A_t\) means the action performed better than predicted. PPO increases its likelihood subject to a clipped update objective \cite{JohnSchulmanFilipWolskiPrafullaDhariwalAlecRadfordOlegKlimov.2017}. GAE builds advantage estimate \(\hat A_t\) from temporal-difference residuals \(\delta_t^{\mathrm{TD}}\), which compare a one-step reward and next value with the current value:
\begin{equation}
\delta_t^{\mathrm{TD}}=R_{t+1}+\gamma v_{t+1}-v_t,\qquad
\hat A_t=\delta_t^{\mathrm{TD}}+\gamma\lambda\hat A_{t+1}.
\label{eq:gae}
\end{equation}
GAE decay \(\lambda\) trades reliance on short value-based predictions against longer realized trajectories. Backpropagation through time (BPTT) is different. It unfolds recurrent state \(h_t\) across a sequence and propagates gradients through the repeated network operations \cite[Chapter~10]{Goodfellow-et-al-2016}. Longer unrolls require more computation and can cause vanishing or exploding gradients, meaning gradients become too small or too large for stable learning.

Recurrent state \(h_t\) carries observation history during trajectory collection. GAE then computes advantage targets \(\hat A_t\) backward through collected rewards \(R_t\) and value estimates \(v_t\), stopping its recursion at episode boundaries. Ordinary time limits use value bootstrapping, meaning that estimated future value is retained at truncation. It uses the actual final observation \(o_t\), not the automatically reset episode's observation.

Optimization replays bounded trajectory segments with their recurrent starting states \(h_t\). BPTT is limited by the available segment, whereas advantage targets \(\hat A_t\) have already been calculated over the rollout. This separates recurrent gradient propagation from reward attribution. Truncation controls computational cost but can still affect which temporal relationships are learned.

\subsubsection{Training Dynamics and Stability}
\label{sec:saa-stability}

Recurrent policy training balances exploration against convergence. Learning rate \(\alpha_{\mathrm{lr}}\), entropy coefficient \(c_H\), PPO clipping width \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{PPO}}\), and action scale \(\kappa_t\) use configurable hold--ramp--hold schedules, with constant values when endpoints coincide. Policy entropy \(H(\pi_\theta)\) measures uncertainty in action distribution \(\pi_\theta\). Its coefficient \(c_H\) encourages exploration rather than premature commitment. PPO clipping width \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{PPO}}\) limits the probability-ratio changes rewarded by its surrogate objective \cite{JohnSchulmanFilipWolskiPrafullaDhariwalAlecRadfordOlegKlimov.2017}. Validation covers every asset and validation-block pair from cash-only starts with deterministic actions and fresh recurrent states \(h_t\). Training normalization statistics are synchronized before each sweep, while evaluation statistics remain frozen. A separate transfer risk arises if the frozen SAA observes accounts changed by the hierarchical PAA's trades. Shadow portfolios prevent this direct interference (Section~\ref{sec:info-flow}).

\subsection{Layer 2: The Portfolio Allocator Agent}
\label{sec:paa}

The hierarchical PAA learns cross-sectional allocation from market features, live account information, and the frozen SAA's outputs. Self-attention compares asset representations at the same date rather than carrying its own recurrent state. This section follows that information flow: token construction (Section~\ref{sec:paa-state}), integration of the SAA through shadow accounts (Section~\ref{sec:info-flow}), action execution (Section~\ref{sec:paa-action}), and network processing (Section~\ref{sec:paa-network}).

\subsubsection{State Space: Tokenizing the Asset and Portfolio Universe}
\label{sec:paa-state}

The hierarchical PAA observes all \(N=11\) assets at once. The environment and SAA signal wrapper supply flattened observation \(o_t^{\mathrm{paa}}\). A tokenizer converts selected fields into learned vectors called tokens. Asset tokens \(\mathbf T_t^{(i)}\) and portfolio token \(\mathbf T_t^{\mathrm{pf}}\) form the sequence

\begin{equation}
\operatorname{Tokenizer}(o_t^{\text{paa}}) = \big(\mathbf{T}^{(1)}_t,\, \dots,\, \mathbf{T}^{(N)}_t,\, \mathbf{T}^{\text{pf}}_t\big), \qquad \mathbf{T}^{(i)}_t, \mathbf{T}^{\text{pf}}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}}}.
\label{eq:paa-token-sequence}
\end{equation}

Embedding width \(d_{\mathrm{model}}=64\) follows standard Transformer notation \cite{vaswani2017}. Separate learned linear projections map different raw asset and portfolio inputs to this common width. Figure~\ref{fig:paa-arch} lists these inputs before projection and shows where they enter the encoder-only hierarchical PAA. The definitions below specify their values before running observation normalization.

Each full-feature asset input \(\mathbf A_t^{(i)}\in\mathbb R^{32}\) contains 26 market indicators and six account or policy quantities:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf A_t^{(i)}=
\big[\mathbf f_t^{\mathrm{mkt},(i)},a_t^{(i)},\rho_t^{(i)},
w_t^{\mathrm{live},(i)},w_{t-1}^{(i)},S_t^{\mathrm{sh},(i)},M_t^{\mathrm{sh},(i)}\big].
\label{eq:paa-asset-inputs}
\end{equation}
Market vector \(\mathbf f_t^{\mathrm{mkt},(i)}\) contains log return \(r_t^{(h)}\), relative-return feature \(z_{\mathrm{rel},t}^{(h)}\), volatility-breakout feature \(z_{\mathrm{vol},t}^{(h)}\), volume intensity \(I_t^{(h)}\), and relative volume \(v_t^{(h)}\) at each horizon \(h\in\{1,5,21\}\) (Equations~\eqref{eq:feature-returns}--\eqref{eq:feature-relative-volume}). It also contains the four bar features \(\psi_t^{\mathrm{range}},\psi_t^{\mathrm{close}},\psi_t^{\mathrm{gap}},\psi_t^{\mathrm{wick}}\) (Equation~\eqref{eq:feature-bars}), ATR feature \(\psi_t^{\mathrm{ATR}}\), RSI feature \(\psi_t^{\mathrm{RSI}}\), and band-width feature \(\psi_t^{\mathrm{BB}}\) (Equations~\eqref{eq:feature-atr}, \eqref{eq:feature-rsi}, and~\eqref{eq:feature-bb}). Fisher correlation \(F_t\), standardized beta \(\beta_t^z\), and dispersion \(D_t^z\) follow Equations~\eqref{eq:feature-fisher}, \eqref{eq:feature-beta}, and~\eqref{eq:feature-dispersion}. Fractional-price feature \(\phi_t\) follows Equation~\eqref{eq:feature-ffd}. This gives \(3\cdot5+4+3+3+1=26\) market inputs.

The SAA's scaled request \(a_t^{(i)}\) follows Equation~\eqref{eq:saa-action}. Live weight \(w_t^{\mathrm{live},(i)}\) describes the hierarchical PAA's actual account. Recorded target weight \(w_{t-1}^{(i)}\) is its preceding softmax request from Equation~\eqref{eq:paa-softmax}. For live cash \(c_t\), shares \(x_t^{(i)}\), and prices \(P_t^{(i)}\), live account value \(V_t\) and live weights \(\mathbf w_t^{\mathrm{live}}\) are
\begin{equation}
V_t=c_t+\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_t^{(i)}P_t^{(i)},\qquad
w_t^{\mathrm{live},(0)}=\frac{c_t}{V_t},\qquad
w_t^{\mathrm{live},(i)}=\frac{x_t^{(i)}P_t^{(i)}}{V_t}.
\label{eq:paa-live-weights}
\end{equation}
Cash uses index zero. Shadow holding fraction \(\rho_t^{(i)}\) instead describes the isolated SAA account. For shadow cash \(c_t^{\mathrm{sh},(i)}\), shares \(x_t^{\mathrm{sh},(i)}\), and account value \(U_t^{(i)}\),
\begin{equation}
U_t^{(i)}=c_t^{\mathrm{sh},(i)}+x_t^{\mathrm{sh},(i)}P_t^{(i)},\qquad
\rho_t^{(i)}=\frac{x_t^{\mathrm{sh},(i)}P_t^{(i)}}{U_t^{(i)}}.
\label{eq:shadow-exposure}
\end{equation}
Shadow Sortino \(S_t^{\mathrm{sh},(i)}\) and maximum drawdown \(M_t^{\mathrm{sh},(i)}\) summarize that account's risk. Shadow return \(r_t^{\mathrm{sh},(i)}\) includes costs and cash carry:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
r_t^{\mathrm{sh},(i)}&=\frac{U_t^{(i)}}{U_{t-1}^{(i)}}-1,\\
S_t^{\mathrm{sh},(i)}&=\operatorname{clip}\!\left(
\frac{\bar r_t^{\mathrm{sh},(i)}}{d_t^{-,\mathrm{sh},(i)}},-3,3\right),\qquad
M_t^{\mathrm{sh},(i)}=\operatorname{MDD}_{W_t}(U^{(i)})_t.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:shadow-risk}
\end{equation}
Shadow EMA mean \(\bar r_t^{\mathrm{sh},(i)}\) and downside deviation \(d_t^{-,\mathrm{sh},(i)}\) use Equations~\eqref{eq:sortino-ema} and~\eqref{eq:sortino} with shadow return \(r_t^{\mathrm{sh},(i)}\). Shadow maximum drawdown \(M_t^{\mathrm{sh},(i)}\) uses Equation~\eqref{eq:rolling-drawdown} with shadow value \(U_t^{(i)}\). These definitions do not introduce additional neural outputs.

Portfolio input \(\mathbf P_t\in\mathbb R^{65}\), also listed in Figure~\ref{fig:paa-arch}, combines calendar vector \(\mathbf g_t\) (Equation~\eqref{eq:feature-calendar}), twelve live weights \(\mathbf w_t^{\mathrm{live}}\) (Equation~\eqref{eq:paa-live-weights}), fourteen diagnostics \(\mathbf d_t\), and three eleven-entry vectors:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf P_t=
\big[\mathbf g_t,\mathbf w_t^{\mathrm{live}},\mathbf d_t,
\mathbf w_{t-1}^{\mathrm{risky}},\mathbf S_t^{\mathrm{sh}},\mathbf M_t^{\mathrm{sh}}\big].
\label{eq:paa-portfolio-inputs}
\end{equation}
Recorded risky targets \(\mathbf w_{t-1}^{\mathrm{risky}}\) exclude cash. Shadow risk vectors \(\mathbf S_t^{\mathrm{sh}}\) and \(\mathbf M_t^{\mathrm{sh}}\) collect Equation~\eqref{eq:shadow-risk} across assets. Diagnostic vector \(\mathbf d_t\) preserves the environment's field order:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf d_t=\big(&\alpha_t^{\mathrm{diag}},\mathrm{SR}_t,M_t,\nu_t,\mathrm{TO}_t,C_t^{\mathrm{eff}},
S_t^{\mathrm{stored}},S_t,\\
&\bar r_t,d_t^-,M_t^{\mathrm{stored}},z_t^{\mathrm{rf}},
\alpha_t^{\mathrm{pf,rf}},q_t\big).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:paa-diagnostics}
\end{equation}
Rolling drawdown \(M_t\), Sortino \(S_t\), EMA mean \(\bar r_t\), and downside deviation \(d_t^-\) reuse Equations~\eqref{eq:rolling-drawdown}, \eqref{eq:sortino}, and~\eqref{eq:sortino-ema}. Stored Sortino \(S_t^{\mathrm{stored}}=S_t\) and stored drawdown \(M_t^{\mathrm{stored}}=M_t\) are updated before recording. Despite their ``previous'' field names, they do not provide lagged estimates. Standardized EFFR \(z_t^{\mathrm{rf}}\) and daily cash return \(q_t\) reuse Equation~\eqref{eq:cash-rate}.

Diagnostic excess return \(\alpha_t^{\mathrm{diag}}\) compares net account return \(r_t^{\mathrm{pf}}\) with the custom benchmark's simple return \(r_t^{\mathrm{bench}}\). Portfolio excess log return over cash \(\alpha_t^{\mathrm{pf,rf}}\) instead compares the account with daily cash return \(q_t\):
\begin{equation}
\alpha_t^{\mathrm{diag}}=r_t^{\mathrm{pf}}-r_t^{\mathrm{bench}},\qquad
\alpha_t^{\mathrm{pf,rf}}=
\log\!\big(1+\max(r_t^{\mathrm{pf}},-0.999999)\big)-\log(1+q_t).
\label{eq:paa-return-diagnostics}
\end{equation}
The return floor in \(\alpha_t^{\mathrm{pf,rf}}\) keeps the logarithm finite. The custom benchmark is defined in Section~\ref{sec:paa-action}. Neither excess-return input replaces the SPY-relative reward in Equation~\eqref{eq:reward-excess}.

Rolling Sharpe input \(\mathrm{SR}_t\) measures mean return per unit of total variability. Unlike an annualized excess-over-cash ratio, it uses neither annualization nor cash-rate subtraction. Recent variability \(\nu_t\) measures dispersion over at most twenty returns. For available return count \(n_t\) in risk window \(W_t\), excluding the initialization row,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{SR}_t&=
\begin{cases}
\mu_{W_t}(r^{\mathrm{pf}})_t/\sigma_{W_t}^{(0)}(r^{\mathrm{pf}})_t,
&n_t\geq2,\ \sigma_{W_t}^{(0)}(r^{\mathrm{pf}})_t>0,\\
0,&\text{otherwise},
\end{cases}\\
\nu_t&=
\begin{cases}
\sigma_{\min(20,t)}^{(0)}(r^{\mathrm{pf}})_t,&t\geq2,\\
0,&t<2.
\end{cases}
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:paa-risk-inputs}
\end{equation}
Weight-change turnover \(\mathrm{TO}_t\) measures changes in risky weights between successive observations:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{TO}_t=\operatorname{clip}\!\left(
\frac12\sum_{i=1}^{N}|w_t^{\mathrm{live},(i)}-w_{t-1}^{\mathrm{live},(i)}|,0,1\right).
\label{eq:paa-turnover}
\end{equation}
This turnover input \(\mathrm{TO}_t\) includes price-induced weight drift. It is not executed-notional turnover reported in Chapter~4.

Effective concentration \(C_t^{\mathrm{eff}}\) is the inverse sum of squared risky weights, divided by asset count \(N\):
\begin{equation}
C_t^{\mathrm{eff}}=
\begin{cases}
0,&\sum_{i=1}^{N}(w_t^{\mathrm{live},(i)})^2<\epsilon,\\
\displaystyle\frac{1}{N\sum_{i=1}^{N}(w_t^{\mathrm{live},(i)})^2},&\text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:paa-concentration}
\end{equation}
Risky weights \(w_t^{\mathrm{live},(i)}\) are not renormalized after excluding cash. Concentration \(C_t^{\mathrm{eff}}=1\) corresponds to full equal-weight investment, but cash holdings can make it exceed one. It is not a bounded diversification score. At reset, return and risk summaries are initialized to zero. Initial concentration \(C_0^{\mathrm{eff}}\) follows the starting weights, while rate inputs \(z_0^{\mathrm{rf}}\) and \(q_0\) are already available.

The full flattened observation \(o_t^{\mathrm{paa}}\) contains \(11(32+2)+59=433\) values. Each asset row carries 32 cached market/calendar features, SAA action \(a_t^{(i)}\), and shadow holding fraction \(\rho_t^{(i)}\). The final 59 values form the account block. The tokenizer selects 26 market features for asset input \(\mathbf A_t^{(i)}\) and copies calendar vector \(\mathbf g_t\) from the first asset row into portfolio input \(\mathbf P_t\). Thus raw input dimensions remain \(26+6=32\) and \(6+12+14+3\cdot11=65\), before projection to twelve 64-dimensional tokens.

The hierarchical PAA recomputes its decision from current tokens \(\mathbf T_t^{(i)}\) and \(\mathbf T_t^{\mathrm{pf}}\), without recurrent state. Historical information arrives through engineered features, SAA signals, and running account statistics. Its feed-forward attention operates across the current \(N+1\) tokens, not across past dates.

\subsubsection{Integrating the Single-Asset Agent: Shadow Portfolios}
\label{sec:info-flow}

Section~\ref{sec:saa-stability} identified a structural risk in reusing one trained SAA across eleven assets. If recurrent state \(h_t\) were updated from the live hierarchical PAA account, the hierarchical PAA's trades would alter inputs independently of the SAA's own actions. Figure~\ref{fig:shadow-flow} shows how isolated shadow accounts prevent this direct interference.

One frozen SAA policy is evaluated across assets as a batch, with separate recurrent state \(h_t^{(i)}\) and account value \(U_t^{(i)}\) for each asset and environment. Each account is a standalone single-asset simulator configured with the SAA's own training capital \(c_0\), costs, trade thresholds, and observation settings. It starts in cash on the hierarchical PAA's start date. Its observation follows Equation~\eqref{eq:saa-observation}: CSV-ordered features \(\mathbf f_t\), context vector \(\mathbf m_t\), and final one-hot identity \(\mathbf e_i\). The same dynamically constructed EFFR inputs enter context vector \(\mathbf m_t\) through Equation~\eqref{eq:saa-context}. Saved SAA observation normalization is frozen during inference. An observation-shape mismatch with the checkpoint raises an error.

Deterministic SAA action \(\hat a_t^{(i)}\) is multiplied by terminal training scale \(\kappa_{\mathrm{end}}\) in float32 and clipped as in Equation~\eqref{eq:saa-action}. Scaled request \(a_t^{(i)}\) enters asset input \(\mathbf A_t^{(i)}\) and is executed in the isolated account. Shadow holding fraction \(\rho_t^{(i)}\), defined in Equation~\eqref{eq:shadow-exposure}, describes the account before that request. Shadow Sortino \(S_t^{\mathrm{sh},(i)}\) and maximum drawdown \(M_t^{\mathrm{sh},(i)}\) follow Equation~\eqref{eq:shadow-risk}. Immutable shadow-value snapshots \(U_t^{(i)}\) retain losses from transaction costs. These environment-maintained summaries enter both token types rather than becoming extra neural outputs.

Shadow execution computes the next-date account state, but its observation is withheld until the live hierarchical PAA reaches that date. SAA request \(a_t^{(i)}\), holding fraction \(\rho_t^{(i)}\), and risk summaries \(S_t^{\mathrm{sh},(i)},M_t^{\mathrm{sh},(i)}\) therefore use no next-date information. Exported balances and SAA costs are proportionally scaled to the hierarchical PAA's reporting capital without re-executing trades at that capital level. SAA costs reduce only the shadow accounts, not live account value \(V_t\). Recurrent states \(h_t^{(i)}\) reset independently at each environment's episode boundary.

This isolation prevents the hierarchical PAA's trades from directly changing SAA account inputs or recurrent state \(h_t^{(i)}\). It preserves single-asset accounting, but does not guarantee identical observation distributions across initialization, normalization, or market conditions. The hierarchical PAA can discount request \(a_t^{(i)}\), holding fraction \(\rho_t^{(i)}\), and shadow risk summaries \(S_t^{\mathrm{sh},(i)},M_t^{\mathrm{sh},(i)}\). It receives neither recurrent state \(h_t^{(i)}\) nor an explicit uncertainty estimate. Figure~\ref{fig:shadow-flow} makes this transfer boundary explicit.

\subsubsection{Action Space: Softmax Allocation with a Fixed Cash Anchor}
\label{sec:paa-action}

The hierarchical PAA's actor parameterizes a Gaussian distribution over risky-asset logits \(\mathbf z_t\), allocation scores before softmax. Sampled training actions and deterministic evaluation means are clipped to action space \(\mathcal A_{\mathrm{paa}}=[-b,b]^N\), with logit bound \(b=5\) by default. Cash logit \(z_t^{(0)}\equiv0\) is prepended. A stable softmax converts logits \(z_t^{(j)}\) to positive target weights \(w_t^{(j)}\) summing to one:

\begin{equation}
w_t^{(j)} = \frac{\exp\big(z_t^{(j)} - \max_k z_t^{(k)}\big)}{\sum_{k=0}^{N} \exp\big(z_t^{(k)} - \max_k z_t^{(k)}\big)}, \qquad j = 0, \dots, N.
\label{eq:paa-softmax}
\end{equation}

Anchoring cash logit \(z_t^{(0)}\) removes the common-shift redundancy of an unconstrained softmax. Adding the same constant to every logit leaves target weights \(\mathbf w_t\) unchanged. Risky logits \(z_t^{(i)}\) are interpreted relative to cash. Zero risky logits \(z_t^{(i)}=0\) produce equal target weights \(w_t^{(j)}=1/(N+1)\), not a hold action. Maximum cash target \(w_{\max}^{(0)}=1/(1+Ne^{-b})\) is approximately \(93.10\%\) for asset count \(N=11\) and logit bound \(b=5\). Exact all-cash and single-asset targets are not reachable with finite logits \(\mathbf z_t\). Target reachability does not imply immediate execution.

Target weights \(\mathbf w_t\) are not applied instantaneously. Soft execution moves live weights \(\mathbf w_t^{\mathrm{live}}\) part-way toward them. Desired weight change \(\Delta\mathbf w_t\) and soft weight change \(\Delta\mathbf w_t^{\mathrm{soft}}\) are

\begin{equation}
\Delta\mathbf w_t=\mathbf w_t-\mathbf w_t^{\mathrm{live}},\qquad
\Delta\mathbf w_t^{\mathrm{soft}}=
\eta_{\mathrm{exec}}\Delta\mathbf w_t\odot
\mathbb{1}[|\Delta\mathbf w_t|\geq\delta].
\label{eq:paa-soft-execution}
\end{equation}

Execution step size \(\eta_{\mathrm{exec}}\in(0,1]\) sets the partial move. Deadband \(\delta\) suppresses changes below a threshold. Elementwise product \(\odot\) and the indicator apply to every weight, including cash. Negative provisional weights are clipped to zero and renormalized before share orders are formed. Sales are inventory-limited and precede purchases. Purchases can be scaled down to cover costs from available cash. Minimum-share thresholds can suppress individual orders. Soft weight change \(\Delta\mathbf w_t^{\mathrm{soft}}\) is therefore an intermediate request, not the final filled change. Reward \(R_t\) uses SPY buy-and-hold value \(B_t\) in Equation~\eqref{eq:reward-excess}. Vector gap \(G_t^{\mathrm{paa}}\) in Equation~\eqref{eq:execution-gap-paa} remains diagnostic only.

Three reference accounts must be distinguished. Reward counterfactual value \(B_t\) comes from fully invested SPY buy-and-hold, paying initial transaction cost and then retaining the position. Comparison value \(B_t^{\mathrm{start}}\) retains the live account's initial allocation without further trading. Custom benchmark value \(B_t^{\mathrm{bench}}\) starts at \(45\%\) SPY, \(20\%\) Gold, \(10\%\) EWJ, \(10\%\) EWG, and \(5\%\) each in Crude, EWQ, and EWT. Its simple return \(r_t^{\mathrm{bench}}=B_t^{\mathrm{bench}}/B_{t-1}^{\mathrm{bench}}-1\) supplies diagnostic excess return \(\alpha_t^{\mathrm{diag}}\) in Equation~\eqref{eq:paa-return-diagnostics}. Only SPY reference value \(B_t\) enters excess-return reward \(\Delta\ell_t\).

\subsubsection{Network Architecture: Shared Attention with Private Heads}
\label{sec:paa-network}

The hierarchical PAA is an \emph{encoder-only, identity-aware, cross-sectional Transformer actor--critic}. Encoder-only means that attention transforms current asset tokens \(\mathbf T_t^{(i)}\) and portfolio token \(\mathbf T_t^{\mathrm{pf}}\) without a decoder generating an output sequence. Each decision requests one joint target vector \(\mathbf w_t\), not a sequence of future trades. Attention operates across assets and the portfolio at the current date. The hierarchical PAA has no recurrent temporal state.

Figure~\ref{fig:paa-arch} links the feature groups in Equations~\eqref{eq:paa-asset-inputs} and~\eqref{eq:paa-portfolio-inputs} to shared attention and private actor and critic processing. For each attention head, query matrix \(\mathbf Q\), key matrix \(\mathbf K\), and value matrix \(\mathbf V\) are learned projections of the current token representations. Query--key similarity determines which value vectors are combined \cite{vaswani2017}:
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{Attention}(\mathbf Q,\mathbf K,\mathbf V)=
\operatorname{softmax}\!\left(\frac{\mathbf Q\mathbf K^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)\mathbf V.
\label{eq:paa-attention}
\end{equation}
Key width \(d_k=16\) scales the similarities. Multiple heads learn different comparisons and their outputs are combined. The shared encoder uses layer count \(L_{\mathrm{enc}}=2\), head count \(H_{\mathrm{attn}}=4\), embedding width \(d_{\mathrm{model}}=64\), and feed-forward width \(d_{\mathrm{ff}}=128\). Normalization precedes each residual block, with zero Transformer dropout and a Gaussian error linear unit (GELU), a smooth nonlinear activation. A residual connection adds each transformed representation to its input, helping information propagate. One private encoder layer per output branch adapts the shared representation to policy and value estimation.

Learned identity embedding \(\mathbf e_i^{\mathrm{PAA}}\) is added through trainable scale \(\gamma_{\mathrm{id}}\), initialized at \(0.1\), followed by layer normalization. The hierarchical PAA is therefore identity-aware, not a rule over anonymous interchangeable tokens. Shared attention is permutation-equivariant when token content and identity move together, meaning that reordering tokens reorders their encoded representations. The complete policy also uses ordered portfolio fields and fixed output positions. Mean pooling, the arithmetic average of encoded asset tokens, therefore does not establish permutation invariance or guarantee that arbitrary input reordering gives correctly reordered allocations.

The actor's private layer processes the eleven encoded asset tokens and averages their representations before producing risky logits \(\mathbf z_t\). The critic's private layer processes only the encoded portfolio token and estimates scalar value \(v_t\). Both branches have already received portfolio-wide information through the shared encoder. This routing does not prevent the actor from using portfolio context or the critic from using asset context. Each private layer receives gradients from its own objective, while both losses still influence the shared encoder.

\subsubsection{Actor-Critic Interaction}

Actor logits \(\mathbf z_t\) and critic value \(v_t\) remain separate (Section~\ref{sec:actor-critic-theory}). The critic influences learning through advantage estimates \(\hat A_t\) and shared-encoder gradients, not through target weights \(\mathbf w_t\). A miscalibrated value estimate \(v_t\) can therefore weaken the actor's training signal even though it never enters the trade directly. Initial network-weight scales account for layer width and depth to limit unstable signal propagation through the shared residual paths.
