\section{Conclusion, Future Work, and Limitations}
\label{sec:conclusion}

\subsection{Conclusion}

The principal achievement of PyTrade is a trained Portfolio Allocator Agent (PAA) that outperforms SPY buy-and-hold on mean validation return and on a held-out test block, under simulated trading frictions. Using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), the selected hierarchical agent returns \(29.46\%\) on test, against SPY's \(22.22\%\). The cross-sectional-only agent returns \(31.83\%\), approximately matching equal-weight buy-and-hold's \(31.80\%\), with a maximum drawdown of \(9.51\%\) against that benchmark's \(10.19\%\). These results demonstrate profitable allocation with some capacity to manage downside risk, rather than return maximization alone. They do not establish universal risk-adjusted superiority: the hierarchy's test drawdown is \(24.75\%\), and neither selected agent exceeds the passive market benchmarks on the reported Sharpe ratio (Table~\ref{tab:paa-validation-test}).

This outcome is obtained from open, high, low, and close prices and trading volume (OHLCV), together with Effective Federal Funds Rate (EFFR) information, without news, fundamentals, or order-book data. Stationary, multi-horizon feature engineering and a custom trading simulator provide the infrastructure for cost-aware learning and consistent comparison (Sections~\ref{sec:state} and~\ref{sec:simulator}). Their implementation, alongside validation-only checkpoint selection, is a substantial methodological and engineering contribution.

The original temporal-to-cross-sectional decomposition receives qualified support. The Single-Asset Agent (SAA) and its isolated shadow portfolios function operationally, but their addition does not consistently improve allocation. The hierarchy leads both ablations in val\_02 and maintains a broad plateau of strong validation performance during training; it nevertheless trails the cross-sectional-only agent on aggregate validation and test results (Section~\ref{sec:paa-results-discussion}). Thus the proposed interface neither establishes a general advantage nor completely fails. The thesis demonstrates effective portfolio learning and a viable modular architecture while showing that a useful single-asset policy need not supply an incrementally useful allocation signal.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The evaluation covers four repeatedly consulted validation regimes and one held-out test block. Selecting the best run separately across four seeds per family reveals variability but does not establish statistically significant architectural differences. The randomized-signal control has only one noise realization per evaluation. Moreover, the 60-trading-day purge does not formally cover the maximum fractional-differentiation lookback and subsequent normalization. These constraints limit generalization claims. The reported Sharpe ratios also use raw returns without subtracting cash carry, and validation and test horizons differ.

The environment is long-only: agents cannot borrow and sell an asset to profit directly from a price decline. Observations exclude VIX and order-book information about available bids, offers, and liquidity. Daily closing-price replay therefore represents a restricted market view. Gold and Crude futures use simplified directly held asset accounting. It omits contract multipliers that translate quoted prices into exposure, margin collateral, daily cash settlement, and rollover, the replacement of an expiring contract. These mechanics and their costs matter for real futures trading.

Returns include the configured commission, spread, and market-impact charges (Section~\ref{sec:friction}), but exclude taxes, broker-specific fees beyond those assumptions, and execution slippage not captured by the parametric cost model. High turnover makes these omissions material, particularly for investors in taxable accounts. Cash carry is another approximation: retail investors cannot invest directly at EFFR, a volume-weighted median overnight federal-funds transaction rate reported on an annualized basis. Money-market funds can provide exposure to similar short-term rates, but fees, instrument composition, and implementation differences prevent exact replication of the modeled cash return.

Finally, shadow-account consistency establishes correct bookkeeping, not predictive value or equivalence with standalone SAA evaluation. Transfer includes action, shadow exposure, Sortino, and drawdown, so the comparisons do not identify an action-only contribution. The shadow Sortino return proxy also assigns cash carry the opposite sign from ordinary net return, limiting its interpretation as an investment-risk measure. The cross-sectional-only ablation retains historical features, live statistics, and risk-summary fields for untraded cash accounts. Its success therefore does not show that temporal or shadow-account information is unnecessary. Nor does the comparison isolate attention from the rest of the allocator. The evidence concerns this combined interface, not modular learning in general.

\subsection{Future Work}
\label{sec:future-work}

A promising extension is a PAA informed by several complementary specialists rather than one shared temporal policy. These could include a behaviorally informed agent representing loss aversion or overconfidence, following the perspective reviewed in Section~\ref{sec:modular-composition} \cite{Charkestani.2026}, and a rule-based agent whose mean-reversion signals are derived from regression analysis fitted only on training periods. A behaviorally informed SAA might supply information more distinct from the allocator's technical features; whether that distinction improves performance remains an empirical question. Each specialist should be tested through matched ablations, with isolated accounting and an explicit mechanism for discounting uncertain signals.

The current SAA shares weights across assets, rotating its assigned instrument between episodes and using a learned asset-identity embedding. Training a separate specialist per asset could capture instrument-specific behavior that this shared representation misses, although reduced data per model and greater training cost may increase overfitting. Separately ablating the action, holding fraction, and shadow risk summaries would clarify which transferred quantities contribute useful information. The risk-summary fields should also be explicitly masked when testing a completely shadow-information-free allocator.

Stronger evaluation should combine additional untouched regimes, paired comparisons across more seeds, repeated randomized signals, and cost and tax sensitivity tests. Explicit futures accounting and an investable money-market cash proxy would improve deployment realism. A monolithic end-to-end baseline, not implemented here, should jointly learn timing and allocation under a comparable total training budget, including specialist pretraining. These comparisons would distinguish complementary specialist information from gains already available through engineered features and portfolio-level learning.
