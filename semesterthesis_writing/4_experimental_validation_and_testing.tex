\section{Experimental Validation and Testing}
\label{sec:validation}

Section~\ref{sec:system-design} explained how PyTrade is built and why each design choice was made; this chapter reports what happens when the resulting agents are actually run on data they have never been trained on. Every comparison below follows directly from a claim made in Section~\ref{sec:system-design}. The excess-return reward design (Section~\ref{sec:excess-return}) is only meaningful if the agent it trains is actually shown to beat the $100\%$ B\&H it is scored against, not merely a strawman that is easy to beat. The shadow-portfolio mechanism that injects the Single-Asset Agent's (SAA) signal into the Portfolio Allocator Agent (PAA, Section~\ref{sec:info-flow}) is only justified if removing or corrupting that signal measurably hurts the PAA. The modular decomposition itself (Section~\ref{sec:overview}) is only a defensible answer to this thesis's research question if the resulting two-layer system outperforms both a signal-free cross-sectional model and, budget permitting, a monolithic single-network alternative trained end to end with comparable computational budget. Baselines throughout this chapter are therefore chosen to isolate one specific claim each, and the purged, block-level out-of-sample protocol recalled in Section~\ref{sec:oos-protocol} is what makes any of these comparisons trustworthy rather than an artifact of in-sample memorization.

This chapter is organized in the order its two agents were introduced. Section~\ref{sec:oos-protocol} recaps the evaluation protocol shared by both agents and introduces the validation periods used. Section~\ref{sec:saa-results} reports SAA results on the validation periods. Section~\ref{sec:paa-results} reports the PAA results on both validation and test periods.

\subsection{Out-of-Sample Protocol}
\label{sec:oos-protocol}

Every result in this chapter is reported on the purged, block-level split introduced in Section~\ref{sec:simulator}: repeated purged train/validation cycles carved out of the full 2000--2025 history, each separated from its own preceding training block by a 60-trading-day purge gap, followed by one held-out test block preceded by its own purge gap. Applied to the current dataset, this scheme yields four validation blocks, each spanning a distinct historical regime, and one test block reserved for a single, final evaluation. The distinction between these two evaluation surfaces matters for how the rest of this chapter should be read. The four validation blocks are used iteratively, both for the "peak alpha" checkpoint selection and for every diagnostic plot in this chapter; because they are consulted repeatedly during development, a result reported only on a validation block is evidence of learning, not yet evidence of genuine, unseen-data skill. The test block is touched exactly once, after every design and checkpoint decision has already been made on the validation blocks alone, which is precisely what separates a legitimate out-of-sample result from an inflated, cherry-picked headline number.


Table~\ref{tab:oos-blocks} names each validation block by its calendar date range and summarizes the macroeconomic and financial-market conditions realized within it. This step is necessary because a purged split only guarantees that training and evaluation windows do not overlap in time; it does not guarantee that the resulting blocks sample comparable market regimes. An agent evaluated only on calm, trending markets can appear skillful without having learned genuine risk management, since correctly timing entries and exits is easier when volatility and drawdowns are historically low. Conversely, an agent evaluated on a block dominated by a single macroeconomic shock can be penalized for defensive behavior, such as temporarily reducing exposure, that is rational given that shock but is scored harshly by a reward function tuned to reward decisiveness. Naming the realized regime of every block turns this risk into an explicit, auditable property of the evaluation protocol rather than a hidden confound.

Four technical terms recur in Table~\ref{tab:oos-blocks} and are defined here once. \emph{Quantitative easing} (QE) is a central bank's large-scale purchase of government or other securities, undertaken once its short-term policy rate is already near zero, with the aim of lowering long-term interest rates; it matters for an evaluation window because it can suppress volatility and support asset prices independent of the underlying economic performance of any single firm or country. A \emph{yield-curve inversion} occurs when short-maturity government bonds yield more than long-maturity bonds, the reverse of the usual relationship; it is a widely cited leading indicator of recession, because it reflects an expectation among bond investors that the central bank will need to cut rates in the future in response to a weakening economy. The \emph{VIX} is an index of the market's expectation of near-term S\&P 500 volatility, implied by option prices; a sharp rise in the VIX signals a sudden increase in perceived risk and is commonly referred to as a volatility spike. A \emph{carry trade} is a strategy of borrowing in a currency with a low interest rate to invest in higher-yielding assets denominated in another currency; an \emph{unwind} of such a trade, triggered for instance by a rise in the funding currency's interest rate, forces borrowers to close these positions quickly, producing fast, correlated selling across otherwise unrelated asset classes.

The test block (final row of Table~\ref{tab:oos-blocks}) is deliberately not characterized narratively in advance of evaluation. Naming its dominant events before the single, final evaluation pass described above would risk tempting a post-hoc, narrative-driven justification of whatever result that pass produces, which is precisely what the purged protocol is designed to prevent. Its realized composition is instead reported alongside its results.

Configuration \texttt{01056} earns positive Asset $\alpha$ in 29 of 44 asset-period cells. Its mean Asset $\alpha$ is $8.34\%$, while mean Market $\alpha$ is $7.71\%$. The result is not uniform. Period-average Asset $\alpha$ is $-0.94\%$, $15.19\%$, $20.08\%$, and $-0.99\%$ from val\_00 through val\_03. Val\_02 is especially broad: all eleven assets beat their own passive benchmarks. Val\_03 is the weakest block and has the deepest mean drawdown, $-15.66\%$.

Mean turnover is $3200.31\%$ and mean cost is $0.34\%$ of initial capital. These values are lower than for \texttt{01058}, but they still imply frequent trading. The friction estimate may therefore be optimistic for the less liquid instruments. Cash carry also differs by block. Since the SAA is long-only and can retreat to cash, part of its measured alpha may reflect the prevailing EFFR rather than timing alone.

Val\_02 and val\_03 are retained for the detailed plots. They are the selected model's best and worst blocks by Asset $\alpha$. This contrast shows both the signal's potential and its failure mode. Aggregate plots compare the equal-weight shadow portfolio with uniform buy-and-hold, SPY buy-and-hold, and cash. Asset plots show each shadow portfolio, its own buy-and-hold benchmark, and signed trading volume.

In val\_02, the equal-weight SAA portfolio rises steadily during the first half of the block, then largely preserves its gains through the late-2018 sell-off. It finishes up $19.5\%$, compared with $6.7\%$ for SPY, $2.4\%$ for cash, and a $3.7\%$ loss for uniform asset buy-and-hold. Its maximum drawdown is about $4.4\%$, versus about $20\%$ for SPY and uniform buy-and-hold. The asset panels show that this result is broad rather than attributable to one holding: all eleven agents finish above their own buy-and-hold benchmarks. The largest cumulative agent-minus-benchmark gaps are EWY ($41.56$ percentage points), EWS ($29.92$), EWG ($29.55$), and EWJ ($29.17$); the gaps are smaller for Gold ($2.50$) and SPY ($3.41$). The signed trade bars show repeated position changes, but these are exits and re-entries in a long-only account, not short sales (Section~\ref{sec:saa}). Total transaction costs across the eleven shadow accounts are $\$395.14$.

\subsubsection{Discussion}
\label{sec:saa-results-discussion}

\label{sec:saa-robustness}

The corrected results support a different checkpoint choice. Run \texttt{00233}, config\_01056, has the highest mean Asset $\alpha$, the best mean Sharpe ratio, and the least severe mean maximum drawdown. Run \texttt{00234}, config\_01057, is positive in more asset-period cells and trades less, but its mean Asset $\alpha$ is lower and its mean drawdown is worse. Run \texttt{00239}, config\_01058, is weaker on the main performance measures.

The selected policy remains regime-dependent: its mean Asset $\alpha$ is strongest in val\_01 and val\_02, and slightly negative in val\_00 and val\_03. This pattern is visible in the two graph periods. In val\_02, the SAA ensemble retreats through a sharp benchmark drawdown; in val\_03, its more defensive exposure limits the fall but also misses part of the Gold and German-equity rallies. Gold's agent portfolio still gains about $49\%$ in val\_03, but buy-and-hold gains about $68\%$, leaving a cumulative graph alpha of $-18.73$ percentage points. This is a useful reminder that high absolute return is not the same as successful timing.

These outcomes follow from the SAA's action design in Section~\ref{sec:saa}. Its scalar action requests a bounded change in position, scaled by $\kappa_t$; it is not a target portfolio weight. The agent is long-only, so its defensive response is to reduce or close its holding and earn cash carry, not to short a falling asset. The drawdown-aware reward (Section~\ref{sec:drawdown}) can favor this risk reduction, while Asset $\alpha$ still records the opportunity cost when an asset continues to rally. Market-relative and cross-asset features (Section~\ref{sec:state}) inform each decision, but the SAA cannot reallocate its capital to another instrument.

The PAA can make that cross-sectional choice. Under the shadow-portfolio design (Section~\ref{sec:info-flow}), each frozen SAA copy maintains its own cash, holdings, and recurrent state, isolated from the live PAA portfolio. Its emitted action and shadow holding fraction $\rho_t^{(i)}$ enter the asset token alongside market features and the asset's live PAA weight (Section~\ref{sec:paa-state}). The action and $\rho_t^{(i)}$ provide different context: the first is a requested position change, while the second describes the shadow account's current exposure. The PAA's attention network can weigh both against the other assets and the portfolio token rather than treating an SAA action as an allocation instruction (Section~\ref{sec:paa-network}).

Config\_01056 therefore supplies a useful, but imperfect, timing signal for continued PAA training. Its positive mean Asset $\alpha$ and broad gains in val\_02 justify carrying it forward; the losses and regime variation in val\_03 justify leaving the PAA free to discount it. Run \texttt{00233} is the checkpoint selected for that purpose. The final test block remains untouched until PAA design and checkpoint choices are fixed.

\subsubsection{Results}
\label{sec:paa-results-presentation}

\paragraph{Selection criterion and evaluation conditions.}
Within each run, the saved peak-excess checkpoint is the candidate for comparison. One candidate per model family is then selected by the highest mean excess return over SPY across the four validation blocks:
\begin{equation}
J_{\mathrm{val}} = \frac{1}{4}\sum_{b=0}^{3}\left(R_{\mathrm{PAA},b}-R_{\mathrm{SPY},b}\right),
\qquad R_{k,b}=\frac{V_{k,b}^{\mathrm{end}}}{V_0}-1.
\label{eq:paa-selection}
\end{equation}
Every evaluation starts from \(V_0=\$100{,}000\), so maximizing this criterion is equivalent to maximizing mean dollar excess value. These are cumulative block returns, not the annualized alpha measures used in the preceding SAA tables. Each validation block receives equal weight. Neither test return nor test risk metrics enter selection; only the three validation-selected candidates are reported on the final test block.

All PAA evaluations use deterministic policy actions and a cash-only start. The AR(1) control additionally uses a reproducible noise realization, with separate validation and test seeds. Validation blocks contain 289 or 290 recorded dates; the test block contains 252. Returns are net of simulated transaction costs and include cash carry. The reported Sharpe ratio is \(\sqrt{252}\,\bar r/s(r)\), calculated from daily simple returns after the initialization row; the export does not subtract the risk-free rate. Maximum drawdown (MDD) is reported as a positive loss magnitude. Means of block-level returns, Sharpe ratios, and MDDs are arithmetic summaries, not statistics of one concatenated investment path.

The full-feature comparison retains the same allocator architecture, token dimensions, reward settings, and configured training budget. The hierarchical PAA receives the frozen SAA's action and shadow holding fraction. The control replaces the action source with clipped AR(1) noise (coefficient \(0.9\), innovation standard deviation \(0.15\)), committed through the same shadow-book mechanism. The cross-sectional-only PAA receives zero action and zero shadow holding fraction; its shadow accounts are never traded. Neither ablation runs the frozen SAA.

\subsubsection{Discussion}
\label{sec:paa-results-discussion}

\paragraph{Allocation success is not the same as successful signal transfer.}
The PAAs show positive SPY-relative performance on validation averages and on the final test. This is consistent with the continuous-control motivation in the literature review: allocation decisions can exploit cross-asset opportunities rather than merely predict one asset's next return. It supports the usefulness of the implemented allocator, but does not establish that attention is superior to another allocation architecture, since that component is not separately ablated.

The SAA's incremental contribution is not consistently positive. The selected hierarchy exceeds the random-signal control by \(2.20\) points in mean validation return and \(2.83\) points on test, but trails the cross-sectional-only PAA by \(0.25\) and \(2.37\) points. The four-run full-feature hierarchy also has a lower mean validation excess than the cross-sectional-only family. Positive results without a learned SAA signal show that the market features and live portfolio state already contain sufficient information for profitable allocation in these blocks.

The regime contrast is more informative than a single average. The hierarchy leads both ablations in val\_02, yet substantially trails them in val\_03 (Figures~\ref{fig:paa-hier-val02}--\ref{fig:paa-cross-val03}). This is compatible with a conditionally useful temporal signal, not a uniformly beneficial one. Section~\ref{sec:modular-composition} explicitly distinguishes architectural specialization from successful transfer; the present results preserve that distinction. They do not identify whether the weak blocks arise from the frozen signal, the allocator's use of it, or training variability.

\paragraph{What the shadow portfolios establish.}
The recorded consistency checks for run \texttt{00283} show no missing shadow-action commits, no non-finite portfolio values, and zero discrepancy between injected and independently regenerated SAA signals and shadow values in every block. This supports the operational correctness of the interface in Section~\ref{sec:info-flow}. It does not prove that the transferred signal improves returns or that the shadow observations reproduce the standalone SAA evaluation distribution. Isolation prevents the PAA's trades from directly rewriting the SAA's account state; it cannot remove a weak or regime-dependent policy's forecasting errors.

Section~\ref{sec:paa-state} exposes the SAA action and shadow holding fraction as features, not binding instructions. The PAA can therefore discount them through the attention network (Section~\ref{sec:paa-network}), but that flexibility does not guarantee that PPO learns when to do so. The AR(1) control also demonstrates that smoothness and an evolving shadow book are insufficient evidence of useful temporal information. Its strong val\_03 result is not evidence that random signals predict returns; useful decisions can still arise from its other inputs, with additional variation from training and the noise realization.

\paragraph{Return, risk, and the reward counterfactual.}
The choice of SPY as the excess-return counterfactual (Section~\ref{sec:excess-return}) explains why positive reward-relative performance need not imply the best diversified investment outcome. On test, the hierarchy beats SPY but trails equal-weight buy-and-hold and experiences roughly \(2.7\) times SPY's MDD. The control likewise trails equal-weight buy-and-hold. The cross-sectional-only model nearly matches that benchmark's return and has a slightly smaller MDD, but a lower reported Sharpe. Thus the strongest conclusion concerns net return relative to SPY, not general risk-adjusted dominance over passive investing.

The composition panels are consistent with cross-sectional rotation, the role assigned to attention in Section~\ref{sec:self-attention}. Average risky exposure stays near \(90\)--\(93\%\), while commodity and equity weights change markedly. Nevertheless, exposure is not a complete risk measure: replacing equity with Crude can change concentration and volatility without changing total risky exposure. Grey turnover bars should therefore be read as approximately exposure-neutral reallocations, not risk-neutral trades. The graphs show neither a predominantly cash-timing policy nor uniformly successful diversification.

The drawdown penalties and partial execution of Sections~\ref{sec:drawdown} and~\ref{sec:paa-action} discourage losses and abrupt reallocations; they do not impose a hard portfolio-risk limit. The hierarchical test drawdown illustrates that distinction. Likewise, positive net returns already absorb the configured friction, but turnover of tens of times NAV makes their economic value sensitive to the commission, spread, and impact assumptions in Section~\ref{sec:friction}. The lower cross-sectional-only costs are an additional practical advantage in this comparison, not evidence that the cost model captures all live-trading frictions.

\paragraph{Generalization and strength of evidence.}
Test returns and reported Sharpe ratios exceed each selected model's validation mean, so the final block shows no uniform collapse in performance. However, the hierarchy and control incur larger test drawdowns, and test excess over SPY falls from \(7.76\) to \(7.24\) points and from \(5.56\) to \(4.40\) points. Only the cross-sectional-only model improves both SPY-relative excess and MDD. Higher absolute test returns also coincide with stronger passive returns and a different evaluation horizon. They are therefore not, by themselves, evidence that the policies generalize better than their validation scores suggest.

Validation-only selection preserves the separation required by Section~\ref{sec:oos-protocol}, but choosing the maximum across checkpoints and configurations can still inflate validation performance, the backtest-selection concern discussed by L\'opez de Prado \cite{LopezdePrado.2018}. Run variation is substantial: full-feature hierarchical validation excess ranges from \(0.32\) to \(7.76\) points, and cross-sectional-only excess from \(-0.06\) to \(8.01\). The \(0.25\)-point gap between the selected models is small relative to those ranges. Repeated recorded seeds, different selected seeds, and a single AR(1) realization per evaluation prevent these rankings from being interpreted as statistically established causal effects of the SAA.

\textbf{\textit{TensorBoard figure to add: plot validation/excess\_return\_over\_spy\_abs\_mean against environment steps for the full-feature candidates, mark their selected checkpoints, and include the per-block val\_03 excess curve. The argument is whether the selected mean reflects sustained validation performance or a brief peak masking deterioration in one regime. Do not infer this behavior from the final-checkpoint tables alone.}}

Overall, the evidence supports an effective SPY-relative allocator and a functioning shadow-portfolio interface, but not a general performance benefit from the frozen SAA.
