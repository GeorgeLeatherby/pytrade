\section{Experimental Validation and Testing}
\label{sec:validation}

This chapter evaluates the agents on blocks excluded from parameter fitting. Passive buy-and-hold references test return relative to the reward counterfactual (Section~\ref{sec:excess-return}); signal-free and randomized-signal ablations test whether the frozen specialist adds allocation value (Section~\ref{sec:info-flow}). These comparisons distinguish profitable allocation from a benefit attributable to signal transfer. They follow the purged block protocol, with validation used for selection and the final test excluded from design decisions. Joint end-to-end learning remains a future comparison rather than a result reported here.

This chapter is organized in the order its two agents were introduced. Section~\ref{sec:oos-protocol} recaps the evaluation protocol shared by both agents and introduces the validation periods used. Section~\ref{sec:saa-results} reports SAA results on the validation periods. Section~\ref{sec:paa-results} reports the PAA results on both validation and test periods.

\subsection{Out-of-Sample Protocol}
\label{sec:oos-protocol}

The split in Section~\ref{sec:simulator} provides four historical validation blocks and one final test block, each preceded by a 60-trading-day purge from its preceding training block. A \emph{checkpoint} is a snapshot of learned parameters during training. Validation is consulted repeatedly to select checkpoints and inspect behavior; its results therefore do not constitute a fresh test after model development. Only validation-selected models are reported on the final test, which is reserved until design and selection decisions are fixed.


Table~\ref{tab:oos-blocks} describes the realized market context of each validation interval. Non-overlapping dates do not imply comparable regimes: rising markets can reward persistent exposure, whereas shocks can reveal its downside. Reporting each block separately therefore helps distinguish timing, asset selection, and risk reduction. The event narrative provides context, not proof that a policy responded to a particular announcement.

Four terms recur. \emph{Quantitative easing} (QE) denotes large-scale central-bank securities purchases intended to ease financial conditions \cite{FedAssetPurchasesSeptember2012}. A \emph{yield-curve inversion} means short-maturity government bonds yield more than longer-maturity bonds; the Treasury maturity spread records this relationship \cite{FREDTreasurySpread}. The Chicago Board Options Exchange's \emph{Volatility Index} (VIX) measures near-term S\&P 500 volatility implied by option prices \cite{BISVolatility2018}. A \emph{carry trade} borrows in a low-interest-rate currency to invest in higher-yielding assets elsewhere; closing such positions during stress can amplify currency and asset-price movements \cite{BISCarryTrades2024}.

The test block is not used for narrative-driven model selection. Its portfolio outcomes are reported only after the validation decisions are fixed.

\subsection{Single-Asset Agent Results}
\label{sec:saa-results}


This section compares three final SAA candidates on all four validation blocks. Mean Asset $\alpha$ is annualized agent return minus the same asset's annualized buy-and-hold return; it measures relative performance, not a regression-based market alpha. Maximum drawdown (MDD) is the largest fall from a previous value peak, reported here with a negative sign. Sharpe compares return with total variability, Sortino with downside variability, and Calmar divides annualized return by absolute maximum drawdown. Turnover measures cumulative position-changing activity. These metrics provide secondary checks alongside the selection criterion.

Configuration \texttt{01056} earns positive Asset $\alpha$ in 29 of 44 asset-period cells. Its mean Asset $\alpha$ is $8.34\%$, while mean Market $\alpha$ is $7.71\%$. The result is not uniform. Period-average Asset $\alpha$ is $-0.94\%$, $15.19\%$, $20.08\%$, and $-0.99\%$ from val\_00 through val\_03. Val\_02 is especially broad: all eleven assets beat their own passive benchmarks. Val\_03 is the weakest block and has the deepest mean drawdown, $-15.66\%$.

Mean turnover is $3200.31\%$ and mean cost is $0.34\%$ of initial capital. These values are lower than for \texttt{01058}, but they still imply frequent trading. The friction estimate may therefore be optimistic for the less liquid instruments. Cash carry also differs by block. Since the SAA is long-only and can retreat to cash, part of its measured alpha may reflect the prevailing EFFR rather than timing alone.

Val\_02 and val\_03 are retained for the detailed plots. They are the selected model's best and worst blocks by Asset $\alpha$. This contrast shows both the signal's potential and its failure mode. Aggregate plots compare the equal-weight shadow portfolio with uniform buy-and-hold, SPY buy-and-hold, and cash. Asset plots show each shadow portfolio, its own buy-and-hold benchmark, and signed trading volume.

In val\_02, the equal-weight SAA portfolio rises steadily during the first half of the block, then largely preserves its gains through the late-2018 sell-off. It finishes up $19.5\%$, compared with $6.7\%$ for SPY, $2.4\%$ for cash, and a $3.7\%$ loss for uniform asset buy-and-hold. Its maximum drawdown is about $4.4\%$, versus about $20\%$ for SPY and uniform buy-and-hold. The asset panels show that this result is broad rather than attributable to one holding: all eleven agents finish above their own buy-and-hold benchmarks. The largest cumulative agent-minus-benchmark gaps are EWY ($41.56$ percentage points), EWS ($29.92$), EWG ($29.55$), and EWJ ($29.17$); the gaps are smaller for Gold ($2.50$) and SPY ($3.41$). The signed trade bars show repeated position changes, but these are exits and re-entries in a long-only account, not short sales (Section~\ref{sec:saa}). Total transaction costs across the eleven shadow accounts are $\$395.14$.

\label{sec:saa-results-discussion}

\label{sec:saa-robustness}

Run \texttt{00233}, configuration 01056, has the highest mean Asset $\alpha$, the best mean Sharpe ratio, and the least severe mean maximum drawdown. Run \texttt{00234}, configuration 01057, is positive in more asset-period cells and trades less, but has lower mean alpha and worse mean drawdown. Run \texttt{00239}, configuration 01058, is weaker on the main performance measures.

The selected policy remains regime-dependent: its mean Asset $\alpha$ is strongest in val\_01 and val\_02, and slightly negative in val\_00 and val\_03. This pattern is visible in the two graph periods. In val\_02, the SAA ensemble retreats through a sharp benchmark drawdown; in val\_03, its more defensive exposure limits the fall but also misses part of the Gold and German-equity rallies. Gold's agent portfolio still gains about $49\%$ in val\_03, but buy-and-hold gains about $68\%$, leaving a cumulative graph alpha of $-18.73$ percentage points. This is a useful reminder that high absolute return is not the same as successful timing.

These outcomes follow from the SAA's action design in Section~\ref{sec:saa}. Its scalar action requests a bounded change in position, scaled by $\kappa_t$; it is not a target portfolio weight. The agent is long-only, so its defensive response is to reduce or close its holding and earn cash carry, not to short a falling asset. The drawdown-aware reward (Section~\ref{sec:drawdown}) can favor this risk reduction, while Asset $\alpha$ still records the opportunity cost when an asset continues to rally. Market-relative and cross-asset features (Section~\ref{sec:state}) inform each decision, but the SAA cannot reallocate its capital to another instrument.

The PAA can make that cross-sectional choice. Each frozen SAA maintains isolated cash, holdings, and recurrent state (Section~\ref{sec:info-flow}). Its action and shadow holding fraction enter the asset token alongside live weight and recorded allocator target. Shadow Sortino and drawdown summaries also enter both token types (Section~\ref{sec:paa-state}). The action requests a position change; the other shadow quantities describe accumulated exposure and simulated account performance. Attention can weigh this combined information against the other assets rather than treating the action as an allocation instruction.

Configuration 01056 therefore supplies a useful, but imperfect, timing signal for PAA training. Its positive mean Asset $\alpha$ and broad gains in val\_02 support carrying it forward; val\_03 justifies leaving the allocator free to discount it. Run \texttt{00233} is selected for that purpose. The final test remains excluded until allocator design and checkpoint choices are fixed.


\subsection{Portfolio Allocator Agent Results}
\label{sec:paa-results}

This section evaluates three versions of the Portfolio Allocator Agent (PAA): the hierarchical PAA, an autoregressive random-signal control, and a cross-sectional-only ablation. An \emph{ablation} removes or replaces one component while retaining the surrounding system, so that its contribution can be assessed. Only runs \texttt{00283}--\texttt{00298}, trained under the latest environment implementation, are considered. Results are presented first; their implications for signal transfer and allocation are discussed separately.

The \textbf{hierarchical PAA} is the proposed two-layer system. It receives the frozen SAA's requested position change, shadow holding fraction, and environment-maintained shadow Sortino and drawdown summaries, alongside market features and live allocator information. These quantities describe the specialist's decision and hypothetical account history without requiring the allocator to obey it (Section~\ref{sec:info-flow}).

The \textbf{autoregressive random-signal control} replaces the learned SAA action with synthetic noise. An autoregressive process of order one, abbreviated AR(1), constructs today's signal from a fraction of yesterday's signal plus a new random disturbance. Here the retained fraction is \(0.9\), and the disturbance has standard deviation \(0.15\); clipping bounds the signal before the action scale is applied. Consequently, the noise is persistent rather than independently redrawn each day. It trades the same shadow accounts, producing corresponding exposure, Sortino, and drawdown histories. This replaces the source of the entire specialist-derived information stream, not merely one isolated scalar.

The \textbf{cross-sectional-only ablation} omits the learned specialist: its action and shadow holding fraction are zero, and its shadow accounts are never traded. The shadow risk-summary fields remain, but describe those untraded cash accounts; they are not separately masked to zero. \emph{Cross-sectional} means comparing assets at the same decision date. The allocator retains market features, live statistics, and recorded targets, including historical summaries. It therefore removes learned specialist behavior, not all temporal or shadow-account information. Neither ablation runs the frozen SAA; all three full-feature variants retain the same architecture and input dimensions.

\subsubsection{Results}
\label{sec:paa-results-presentation}

\paragraph{Selection criterion and evaluation conditions.}
Within each run, the saved peak-excess checkpoint is the candidate for comparison. One candidate per model family is then selected by the highest mean excess return over SPY across the four validation blocks:
\begin{equation}
J_{\mathrm{val}} = \frac{1}{4}\sum_{b=0}^{3}\left(R_{\mathrm{PAA},b}-R_{\mathrm{SPY},b}\right),
\qquad R_{k,b}=\frac{V_{k,b}^{\mathrm{end}}}{V_0}-1.
\label{eq:paa-selection}
\end{equation}
Every evaluation starts from \(V_0=\$100{,}000\), so maximizing this criterion is equivalent to maximizing mean dollar excess value. These are cumulative block returns, not the annualized alpha measures used in the preceding SAA tables. Each validation block receives equal weight. Neither test return nor test risk metrics enter selection; only the three validation-selected candidates are reported on the final test block.

All PAA evaluations use deterministic policy actions, meaning that the policy chooses its predicted mean action rather than sampling an action, and a cash-only start. The AR(1) control additionally uses a reproducible noise realization, with separate validation and test seeds. A seed fixes the random-number sequence used in a run. Validation blocks contain 289 or 290 recorded dates; the test block contains 252. Returns are net of simulated transaction costs and include cash carry derived from the Effective Federal Funds Rate (EFFR), as defined in Section~\ref{sec:saa}. The full-feature comparison retains the same reward settings and configured training budget.

The reported Sharpe ratio is \(\sqrt{252}\,\bar r/s(r)\), where \(\bar r\) is the mean daily simple return and \(s(r)\) its sample standard deviation, calculated after the initialization row. It measures return relative to daily variability; unlike a conventional excess-over-cash Sharpe ratio, this export does not subtract the risk-free rate. Maximum drawdown (MDD) is the largest percentage fall from a preceding portfolio-value peak and is reported as a positive loss magnitude. Net asset value (NAV) is the current value of cash and holdings combined. Turnover sums each day's traded notional divided by that day's NAV, so turnover of \(40\) times NAV describes repeated trading relative to a changing portfolio value, not a \(4000\%\) loss. Risky exposure is the share invested in non-cash assets, including commodities as well as equities. Means of block-level returns, Sharpe ratios, and MDDs are arithmetic summaries of independently reset blocks, not statistics of one concatenated investment path.

\subsubsection{Discussion}
\label{sec:paa-results-discussion}

\paragraph{Allocation success is not the same as successful signal transfer.}
The PAAs show positive SPY-relative performance on validation averages and on the final test. This is consistent with the continuous-control motivation in the literature review: allocation decisions can exploit cross-asset opportunities rather than merely predict one asset's next return. The changing asset weights in Figures~\ref{fig:paa-hier-test}, \ref{fig:paa-ar1-test}, and~\ref{fig:paa-cross-test} illustrate that distinction. Each policy repeatedly chooses both how much risky exposure to hold and which assets should carry it, the two decisions coupled by the live portfolio state and transaction costs in Section~\ref{sec:paa-action}. These results support the usefulness of the implemented allocator, but do not establish that attention is superior to another allocation architecture, since that component is not separately ablated.

The SAA's incremental contribution is not consistently positive. The selected hierarchy exceeds the random-signal control by \(2.20\) points in mean validation return and \(2.83\) points on test, but trails the cross-sectional-only PAA by \(0.25\) and \(2.37\) points. The four-run full-feature hierarchy also has a lower mean validation excess than the cross-sectional-only family. Consequently, beating a random-signal control is not enough to establish useful transfer: the learned signal must also improve on omitting the specialist. Positive results without it show that the retained market features and live portfolio state already permit profitable allocation in these blocks.

The cross-sectional-only result does not imply that temporal information is irrelevant. Multi-horizon returns, fractional differentiation, recorded allocation targets, and running account statistics already summarize history (Sections~\ref{sec:state-returns}, \ref{sec:state-ffd}, and~\ref{sec:paa-state}). Moreover, the hierarchy transfers action, exposure, and shadow risk summaries together. Replacing the action source also changes those account histories, while the zero-signal variant retains the risk-summary fields for untraded cash accounts. The comparison therefore tests the combined frozen-policy/shadow-account interface; it cannot isolate an action-only effect or identify which transferred quantity helps or hurts. This qualifies the recurrent-memory motivation reviewed in Chapter~2 \cite{hausknecht2015deep}: usefulness depends on the information already available and its transfer to the allocation objective.

\paragraph{Regime Correspondence Between the Single-Asset Policy and the Hierarchy.}
The strongest correspondence occurs in val\_02 and val\_03. The selected SAA's period-average Asset \(\alpha\) is \(20.08\%\) in val\_02 and \(-0.99\%\) in val\_03 (Table~\ref{tab:saa-per-asset-metrics}); the hierarchy likewise leads both ablations in val\_02 but substantially trails them in val\_03. Relative to the cross-sectional-only PAA, its return advantage of \(11.73\) points in val\_02 is more than offset by a \(14.21\)-point shortfall in val\_03. Averaging the blocks therefore conceals a large reversal in the value associated with adding the specialist.

The market conditions in Table~\ref{tab:oos-blocks} help interpret this reversal. Val\_02 contains a broad decline followed by recovery, while gold and equities partly diverge. The standalone SAA ensemble largely preserves earlier gains through the sell-off (Figure~\ref{fig:saa-agg-val02}), consistent with the long-only specialist reducing exposure when holding its assigned asset becomes unattractive. The hierarchical PAA also outperforms the passive market references, but follows a different risk path: it rotates between concentrated risky holdings and incurs a \(15.27\%\) drawdown (Figure~\ref{fig:paa-hier-val02}). Its advantage is compatible with useful timing context being combined with asset selection; it is not evidence that the allocator simply reproduces the SAA ensemble's defensive cash positions.

Val\_03 contains a concentration-led rally and two distinct shocks. The SAA's defensive behavior misses part of the Gold and German-equity rallies, leaving seven assets below their own buy-and-hold benchmarks (Section~\ref{sec:saa-results-discussion}). The hierarchy is also weak in this block, but not merely because it holds too much cash: average risky exposure remains \(91.35\%\), and its return is below cash carry as well as the market references. Figure~\ref{fig:paa-hier-val03} instead shows repeated concentration and reversal within the risky portfolio. The cross-sectional-only PAA achieves a higher return and a shallower drawdown through its own rotations (Figure~\ref{fig:paa-cross-val03}). This pattern is consistent with the frozen specialist failing to add reliable guidance under these conditions, but the plots cannot distinguish an unreliable source signal from an allocator that uses it poorly.

The other blocks prevent a simple rule that stronger SAA alpha necessarily means stronger hierarchical allocation. In val\_00, the SAA's mean Asset \(\alpha\) is slightly negative (\(-0.94\%\)), yet the hierarchy earns \(32.52\%\) and almost matches the cross-sectional-only PAA. In val\_01, the SAA's \(15.19\%\) mean Asset \(\alpha\) coexists with the hierarchy's small shortfall to SPY. These measures answer different questions: SAA alpha compares each instrument with its \emph{own} passive benchmark, whereas PAA excess compares a multi-asset portfolio with \emph{SPY}. Successful isolated timing can therefore coexist with an allocation that misses the strongest market opportunity. The SAA supplies a position-change signal, not a ranking of expected cross-asset returns (Sections~\ref{sec:saa} and~\ref{sec:paa-state}).

\paragraph{What the shadow portfolios establish.}
The recorded consistency checks for run \texttt{00283} show no missing shadow-action commits, no non-finite portfolio values, and zero discrepancy between injected and independently regenerated SAA signals and shadow values in every block. This supports the operational correctness of the interface in Section~\ref{sec:info-flow}. Isolation prevents the PAA's trades from directly rewriting the SAA's account state, addressing the specific domain-shift risk identified in Section~\ref{sec:saa-stability}. It does not improve an unreliable policy by itself or prove equivalence with the standalone evaluation.

Indeed, the standalone SAA ensemble and the regenerated PAA-harness reference have different return paths: the former gains approximately \(19.5\%\) in val\_02 (Figure~\ref{fig:saa-agg-val02}), while the latter is nearly flat (Figure~\ref{fig:paa-hier-val02}). Their shared checkpoint identity therefore does not make the two evaluations interchangeable. The regime correspondence discussed above is descriptive evidence, not a decomposition of PAA returns into the previously reported SAA gains. The ablation comparisons within the common PAA harness provide the more direct evidence about transfer.

The transferred action, holding fraction, Sortino, and drawdown are features rather than instructions (Section~\ref{sec:paa-state}). They combine a requested trade with exposure and risk summaries, but do not include hidden state or calibrated forecast confidence. The shadow Sortino input uses the environment-specific return proxy described in Section~\ref{sec:info-flow}; its cash-carry sign differs from ordinary net return, so it is not directly comparable with the financial ratios reported here. Both actor and critic can use these summaries through contextualized tokens (Section~\ref{sec:paa-network}), but that does not guarantee that PPO learns which component to discount. The AR(1) control's strong val\_03 result likewise does not show that random signals predict returns: useful allocation remains possible through other inputs, with variation from training and noise.

\paragraph{Return, risk, and the reward counterfactual.}
The choice of SPY as the excess-return counterfactual (Section~\ref{sec:excess-return}) explains why positive reward-relative performance need not imply the best diversified investment outcome. On test, the hierarchy beats SPY but trails equal-weight buy-and-hold and experiences roughly \(2.7\) times SPY's MDD (Figure~\ref{fig:paa-hier-test}). The control likewise trails equal-weight buy-and-hold (Figure~\ref{fig:paa-ar1-test}). The cross-sectional-only model nearly matches that benchmark's return and has a slightly smaller MDD, but a lower reported Sharpe (Figure~\ref{fig:paa-cross-test}). Its smaller drawdown is attractive to an investor concerned with peak-to-trough losses, while the lower Sharpe cautions against describing it as uniformly more efficient per unit of daily variability. The strongest conclusion concerns net return relative to SPY, not general risk-adjusted dominance over passive investing.

The composition panels are consistent with cross-sectional rotation, the role assigned to attention in Section~\ref{sec:self-attention}. Average risky exposure stays near \(90\)--\(93\%\), while commodity and equity weights change markedly in Figures~\ref{fig:paa-hier-val02}, \ref{fig:paa-hier-val03}, and~\ref{fig:paa-cross-test}. The fixed cash logit of Section~\ref{sec:paa-action} makes cash available as an allocation choice; it does not fix its portfolio weight. Remaining mostly invested is therefore an observed policy outcome. Nevertheless, exposure is not a complete risk measure: replacing equities with Crude can change concentration and volatility without changing total risky exposure. Grey turnover bars are approximately exposure-neutral reallocations, not risk-neutral trades. The plots demonstrate active selection, not uniformly successful diversification.

The drawdown penalties and partial execution of Sections~\ref{sec:drawdown} and~\ref{sec:paa-action} discourage losses and abrupt reallocations; they do not impose a hard portfolio-risk limit. The hierarchy's loss of a substantial part of its test-period peak illustrates that distinction (Figure~\ref{fig:paa-hier-test}). Similarly, the literature review's hybrid-policy studies motivate combining temporal representation and allocation, but report performance under their own data, trading frequencies, and risk settings \cite{JunKevin.2025,GabrielNixonRaj.2025}. Their reported gains cannot establish that this particular frozen interface will be best under the present objective.

Positive net returns already absorb the configured friction, but turnover of tens of times NAV makes their economic value sensitive to the commission, spread, and impact assumptions in Section~\ref{sec:friction}. In val\_03, the hierarchy trades more than the cross-sectional-only model while earning less; on test, the control trades most but has the lowest active-model return. These comparisons show that trading intensity is not a proxy for decision quality (Figures~\ref{fig:paa-hier-val03}, \ref{fig:paa-cross-val03}, and~\ref{fig:paa-ar1-test}). The cross-sectional-only model's lower costs add to its practical appeal here, but do not establish that the simulator captures all live-trading frictions.

\paragraph{Checkpoint stability and seed dependence.}
Figure~\ref{fig:excess_return_over_training} materially strengthens the case for selecting configuration \texttt{10019}. Its broad region of positive validation excess, including five additional checkpoints above \(\$7{,}500\), makes the selected checkpoint less dependent on one isolated favorable evaluation. Remaining above \(\$5{,}000\) after approximately 7.5 million steps provides little evidence of sustained validation deterioration within the observed interval. This contrasts with the post-peak declines of configurations \texttt{10020}--\texttt{10022}, which are consistent with overfitting or unstable learning: further optimization does not preserve their earlier validation performance. The graph does not by itself identify which mechanism causes the decline.

The separate SAA training comparison already warned that a high validation peak can be followed by deterioration (Figure~\ref{fig:saa_train_vs_validation}). The selected hierarchy instead maintains several high-performing checkpoints (Figure~\ref{fig:excess_return_over_training}), showing that this warning does not apply equally to every stage and seed. At the same time, those checkpoints share training data and validation blocks; they are not independent replications. The plateau supports \emph{within-run checkpoint stability}, not universal robustness of the hierarchical architecture or a statistically established SAA contribution. The weaker runs under the same full-feature architecture remain evidence of seed sensitivity.

\paragraph{Generalization and strength of evidence.}
Test returns and reported Sharpe ratios exceed each selected model's validation mean, so the final block shows no uniform collapse in performance (Table~\ref{tab:paa-validation-test}). However, the hierarchy and control incur larger test drawdowns, and test excess over SPY falls from \(7.76\) to \(7.24\) points and from \(5.56\) to \(4.40\) points. Only the cross-sectional-only model improves both SPY-relative excess and MDD. Higher absolute test returns also coincide with stronger passive returns and a different evaluation horizon. They are therefore not, by themselves, evidence that the policies generalize better than their validation scores suggest. The more defensible observation is that each validation-selected model retains a positive net advantage over the chosen SPY counterfactual in one previously unused block, with different risk costs.

Validation-only selection preserves the separation required by Section~\ref{sec:oos-protocol}, but choosing the maximum across checkpoints and configurations can still inflate validation performance, the backtest-selection concern discussed by L\'opez de Prado \cite{LopezdePrado.2018}. Run variation is substantial: full-feature hierarchical validation excess ranges from \(0.32\) to \(7.76\) points, and cross-sectional-only excess from \(-0.06\) to \(8.01\). The \(0.25\)-point gap between the selected models is small relative to those ranges. Four seeds per family reveal variation, but selecting the best seed separately for each family is not a paired statistical comparison. Different selected seeds, one AR(1) realization per evaluation, and one held-out block prevent these rankings from being interpreted as statistically established causal effects of the SAA. The small mean gap and the large regime reversals deserve separate conclusions, rather than one unqualified winner claim.

Overall, the evidence supports effective SPY-relative allocation, a functioning shadow-portfolio interface, and a selected hierarchical run whose high validation performance persists across many checkpoints. It does not establish a general performance benefit from the frozen SAA. The cross-sectional-only model is the most competitive selected model on aggregate validation and test results, while the hierarchy's advantage in val\_02 shows that the comparison remains regime-dependent. The thesis therefore distinguishes three achievements: implementing temporal-to-cross-sectional transfer, learning profitable allocation, and demonstrating the transfer's incremental value. The first two are supported here; the third is conditional rather than universal. This empirical qualification of the proposed decomposition is the central result of the controlled comparison.
