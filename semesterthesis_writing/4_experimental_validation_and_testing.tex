\section{Experimental Validation and Testing}
\label{sec:validation}

Section~\ref{sec:system-design} explained how PyTrade is built and why each design choice was made; this chapter reports what happens when the resulting agents are actually run on data they have never been trained on. Every comparison below follows directly from a claim made in Section~\ref{sec:system-design}. The excess-return reward design (Section~\ref{sec:excess-return}) is only meaningful if the agent it trains is actually shown to beat the passive counterfactual it is scored against, not merely a strawman that is easy to beat. The shadow-portfolio mechanism that injects the Single-Asset Agent's (SAA) signal into the Portfolio Allocator Agent (PAA, Section~\ref{sec:info-flow}) is only justified if removing or corrupting that signal measurably hurts the PAA. The modular decomposition itself (Section~\ref{sec:overview}) is only a defensible answer to this thesis's research question if the resulting two-layer system outperforms both a signal-free cross-sectional model and, budget permitting, a monolithic single-network alternative trained end to end with comparable computational budget. Baselines throughout this chapter are therefore chosen to isolate one specific claim each, and the purged, block-level out-of-sample protocol recalled in Section~\ref{sec:oos-protocol} is what makes any of these comparisons trustworthy rather than an artifact of in-sample memorization.

This chapter is organized in the order its two agents were introduced. Section~\ref{sec:oos-protocol} recaps the evaluation protocol shared by both agents and introduces the validation periods used. Section~\ref{sec:saa-results} reports SAA results on the validation periods. Section~\ref{sec:paa-results} reports the PAA results on both validation and test periods.

\subsection{Out-of-Sample Protocol}
\label{sec:oos-protocol}

Every result in this chapter is reported on the purged, block-level split introduced in Section~\ref{sec:simulator}: repeated purged train/validation cycles carved out of the full 2000--2025 history, each separated from its own preceding training block by a 60-trading-day purge gap, followed by one held-out test block preceded by its own purge gap. Applied to the current dataset, this scheme yields four validation blocks, each spanning a distinct historical regime, and one test block reserved for a single, final evaluation. The distinction between these two evaluation surfaces matters for how the rest of this chapter should be read. The four validation blocks are used iteratively, both for the "peak alpha" checkpoint selection described in Section~\ref{sec:saa-robustness} and for every diagnostic plot in this chapter; because they are consulted repeatedly during development, a result reported only on a validation block is evidence of learning, not yet evidence of genuine, unseen-data skill. The test block is touched exactly once, after every design and checkpoint decision has already been made on the validation blocks alone, which is precisely what separates a legitimate out-of-sample result from an inflated, cherry-picked headline number.


Table~\ref{tab:oos-blocks} names each validation block by its calendar date range and summarizes the macroeconomic and financial-market conditions realized within it. This step is necessary because a purged split only guarantees that training and evaluation windows do not overlap in time; it does not guarantee that the resulting blocks sample comparable market regimes. An agent evaluated only on calm, trending markets can appear skillful without having learned genuine risk management, since correctly timing entries and exits is easier when volatility and drawdowns are historically low. Conversely, an agent evaluated on a block dominated by a single macroeconomic shock can be penalized for defensive behavior, such as temporarily reducing exposure, that is rational given that shock but is scored harshly by a reward function tuned to reward decisiveness. Naming the realized regime of every block turns this risk into an explicit, auditable property of the evaluation protocol rather than a hidden confound.

Four technical terms recur in Table~\ref{tab:oos-blocks} and are defined here once. \emph{Quantitative easing} (QE) is a central bank's large-scale purchase of government or other securities, undertaken once its short-term policy rate is already near zero, with the aim of lowering long-term interest rates; it matters for an evaluation window because it can suppress volatility and support asset prices independent of the underlying economic performance of any single firm or country. A \emph{yield-curve inversion} occurs when short-maturity government bonds yield more than long-maturity bonds, the reverse of the usual relationship; it is a widely cited leading indicator of recession, because it reflects an expectation among bond investors that the central bank will need to cut rates in the future in response to a weakening economy. The \emph{VIX} is an index of the market's expectation of near-term S\&P 500 volatility, implied by option prices; a sharp rise in the VIX signals a sudden increase in perceived risk and is commonly referred to as a volatility spike. A \emph{carry trade} is a strategy of borrowing in a currency with a low interest rate to invest in higher-yielding assets denominated in another currency; an \emph{unwind} of such a trade, triggered for instance by a rise in the funding currency's interest rate, forces borrowers to close these positions quickly, producing fast, correlated selling across otherwise unrelated asset classes.

The test block (final row of Table~\ref{tab:oos-blocks}) is deliberately not characterized narratively in advance of evaluation. Naming its dominant events before the single, final evaluation pass described above would risk tempting a post-hoc, narrative-driven justification of whatever result that pass produces, which is precisely what the purged protocol is designed to prevent. Its realized composition is instead reported alongside its results.

\subsection{Single-Asset Agent Results}
\label{sec:saa-results}

This section reports results for the SAA across all four validation blocks. Three questions structure the presentation: does the agent do anything other than imitate a passive strategy, does it do so consistently across assets and time, and is whatever skill it shows attributable to genuine learning rather than to memorizing the training blocks? Section~\ref{sec:saa-results-presentation} presents the per-asset metrics table and the shadow-portfolio inference graphs; Section~\ref{sec:saa-results-discussion} interprets these results against the SAA design choices of Section~\ref{sec:saa}.

\subsubsection{Results}
\label{sec:saa-results-presentation}

Table~\ref{tab:saa-per-asset-metrics} reports the resulting metrics for every (asset, validation block) pair, together with the per-period average, labeled $\text{\O}$, and the four-period macro average in the final row. All return-based quantities are annualized compound growth rates. Asset $\alpha$ is the agent's annualized return minus that same asset's own annualized buy-and-hold return, the counterfactual defined in Section~\ref{sec:excess-return}. Market $\alpha$ instead subtracts SPY's annualized buy-and-hold return, so it scores performance against one common, asset-agnostic benchmark rather than against each instrument's own passive baseline. Turnover sums the absolute day-over-day change in the agent's own action, since the SAA's continuous scalar output is itself interpreted as a target position (Section~\ref{sec:saa}); Cost expresses cumulative transaction costs (Section~\ref{sec:friction}) as a percentage of initial capital. Two structural constraints from Section~\ref{sec:saa} bear directly on how these numbers should be read. First, the SAA trades exactly one, randomly assigned asset per episode and never observes any other instrument's own price, position, or action; its narrowly defined state $s_t^{\text{saa}} = (p_t, c_t, x_t)$ is confined to that one asset's own account. Its feature vector $\mathbf{f}_t$ is not, however, blind to the broader market: it already carries SPY-relative and cross-sectional statistics, the market-relative return \texttt{z\_rel\_log\_close} (Section~\ref{sec:state-returns}) and the correlation, beta, and dispersion features \texttt{z\_fisher\_corr\_spy}, \texttt{z\_beta\_spy}, and \texttt{z\_market\_dispersion} (Section~\ref{sec:state}), each computed against SPY or across the full eleven-asset universe. Second, its action space is long-only: a sell request is filled only out of existing inventory, so the agent can retreat into cash but cannot take a short position.

The four-period macro average shows the SAA's actions performing better than passively holding its assigned instrument, but only modestly so. Average Asset $\alpha$ is $0.96\%$ and average Market $\alpha$ is $0.34\%$, both positive but an order of magnitude smaller than the return dispersion visible within any single period. Across all 44 (asset, period) cells in Table~\ref{tab:saa-per-asset-metrics}, 24 show a negative Asset $\alpha$ against 20 positive ones; the positive macro average is carried by a small number of large outliers, Crude in val\_00 ($+29.82\%$) and val\_03 ($+22.63\%$), EWT in val\_00 ($+15.22\%$), and EWQ in val\_01 ($+12.74\%$).

Period-average turnover ranges from $4581.29\%$ (val\_02) to $5413.36\%$ (val\_00). Dividing the four-period macro Cost ($0.46\%$ of capital) by the corresponding Turnover ($5050.90\%$) gives an average round-trip cost of about $0.9$ basis points per dollar traded, consistently across all four blocks (roughly $0.83$ to $1.00$ basis points).

The risk-free carry the SAA earns whenever it holds cash is not constant across validation blocks. The environment credits idle cash with a daily carry derived from the Effective Federal Funds Rate (EFFR, Section~\ref{sec:saa}), and this rate differed sharply across the four blocks summarized in Table~\ref{tab:oos-blocks}: val\_00 sits at a cyclical policy-rate peak of $5.25\%$; val\_01 falls inside the near-zero-rate regime that its own QE3 episode presupposes; val\_02 is a rate-hiking block; and val\_03 opens at an elevated rate before the Federal Reserve begins cutting.

Figures~\ref{fig:saa-agg-val00}--\ref{fig:saa-assets-val02-p2} plot the trained checkpoint's shadow-portfolio inference output (Section~\ref{sec:info-flow}) for val\_00 and val\_02, the best- and worst-performing periods in Table~\ref{tab:saa-per-asset-metrics} by period-average Market $\alpha$. Each aggregate figure indexes the combined agent portfolio (equal-weight across the eleven per-asset shadow sub-portfolios), SPY B\&H, and 100\% Cash (EFFR) to 100 at the start of the episode. Each asset-specific figure pairs every instrument's Agent and B\&H index curves with its signed daily trading volume; the second page of each period additionally reports a per-asset Agent$-$B\&H alpha ranking and a transaction-cost breakdown, both computed as cumulative, non-annualized figures over the full evaluation episode rather than the annualized quantities in Table~\ref{tab:saa-per-asset-metrics}.

In val\_00 (Figure~\ref{fig:saa-agg-val00}), the combined agent portfolio reaches $+27.7\%$ by the end of the episode, ahead of SPY B\&H ($+15.1\%$) and of holding 100\% cash at the prevailing EFFR rate ($+4.1\%$); the agent curve shows two intermediate drawdowns, near trading day 105--115 and 120--130, before resuming its uptrend. Figures~\ref{fig:saa-assets-val00-p1} and~\ref{fig:saa-assets-val00-p2} show this aggregate gain is uneven across instruments: the per-episode Agent$-$B\&H alpha ranking is led by Crude ($+34.75\%$) and EWT ($+17.93\%$) and is most negative for EWQ ($-13.84\%$) and EWS ($-8.56\%$). Cumulative transaction costs over the eleven-asset evaluation total $\$589.06$ ($0.1$ bps).

\subsubsection{Discussion}
\label{sec:saa-results-discussion}

The positive macro Asset $\alpha$ reported above should not be read as proof of a generally reliable timing edge; it is closer to an average of many small losses and a few large wins than to a consistent tendency to beat buy-and-hold. What the agent lacks is not market awareness but market \emph{agency}: nothing in its action space lets it act on the market-relative and cross-sectional features of Section~\ref{sec:state} beyond resizing the one position it already holds.

Performance is also sharply regime-dependent, and the direction of that dependence is informative. Table~\ref{tab:oos-blocks} characterizes val\_00 as the calmest, most uniformly trending block in the evaluation set, precisely the regime least likely to penalize a policy for simply staying invested; the SAA's strongest period averages, Sharpe $1.65$, Asset $\alpha$ $+2.80\%$, Market $\alpha$ $+10.68\%$, are recorded there, consistent with the sustained uptrend of the combined agent portfolio in Figure~\ref{fig:saa-agg-val00}. Val\_02, by contrast, covers a broad, correlated equity decline; every per-asset Market $\alpha$ in that block is negative except Crude's, the period-average Market $\alpha$ falls to $-9.06\%$, and the period-average Sharpe and Sortino both turn negative ($-0.30$, $-0.35$), matching the shared drawdown of the agent portfolio and SPY B\&H visible in Figure~\ref{fig:saa-agg-val02}. This asymmetry is not simply a matter of the agent failing to notice the broader regime: its own \texttt{z\_market\_dispersion}, \texttt{z\_fisher\_corr\_spy}, and \texttt{z\_beta\_spy} features (Section~\ref{sec:state}) are precisely designed to flag whether today's move is broad and correlated or idiosyncratic to its own instrument. What it cannot do is act on that distinction beyond resizing its own single position: even a policy that correctly infers a broad sell-off has no lever to rotate into a less-affected instrument or into a fully diversified defensive stance, since it is confined to one asset for the whole episode (Section~\ref{sec:saa}). A calm, trending block rewards this narrow lever; a correlated shock exposes its limits.

The gap between Asset $\alpha$ and Market $\alpha$ exposes a second, related limitation. In val\_01, Crude shows a positive Asset $\alpha$ of $+7.55\%$, achieved by avoiding much of Crude's own $-5.39\%$ buy-and-hold decline, yet its Market $\alpha$ is $-15.14\%$, since SPY itself returned $17.31\%$ over the same block. The same pattern recurs for Gold in val\_01 (Asset $\alpha$ $-0.82\%$, Market $\alpha$ $-20.24\%$) and for EWY in the same block (Asset $\alpha$ $-4.79\%$, Market $\alpha$ $-14.22\%$). In each case, correctly avoiding a weak asset's own decline is not the same as being invested where the period's actual return was. Because the SAA cannot reallocate out of a mediocre instrument into a stronger one within an episode (Section~\ref{sec:saa}), a defensible per-asset decision can still coincide with a large opportunity cost relative to the broader market. Closing exactly this gap, reallocating capital across instruments rather than only timing one, is the PAA's task (Section~\ref{sec:paa-results}), and it is also why the PAA needs more than a single per-asset scalar to work from (Section~\ref{sec:paa-state}).

Turnover offers a further, behavioral caveat, distinct from any training diagnostic reported later in Section~\ref{sec:saa-diagnostics}. Period-average turnover varies only narrowly across val\_00 and val\_02, a narrow band given how differently those two blocks behaved on every other metric. A policy trading adaptively might be expected to modulate its activity with the opportunity available; instead, the SAA rebalances at a broadly similar cadence, roughly forty to sixty times its own capital per 252-step episode, regardless of regime. Read against this turnover, the realized cost of about $0.9$ basis points per dollar traded is plausible for the most liquid member of the universe, SPY, but is optimistic once averaged across seven single-country ETFs and two commodity-linked instruments that are typically quoted with materially wider effective spreads in practice, especially at a trading cadence this high, where each daily rebalance is no longer a negligible fraction of a less liquid instrument's own average daily volume. The friction model (Section~\ref{sec:friction}) applied here therefore likely understates the true cost of a policy that trades this actively. Its market-impact coefficient, $\text{impact\_coeff}=0.0001$ in \texttt{config\_01058.json}, is nearly two orders of magnitude smaller than the $0.0117$ used in \texttt{config\_10014.json}, the actual PAA configuration paired with this SAA checkpoint (\texttt{saa\_run\_id "00239"}, \texttt{saa\_config\_id "01058"}); that same PAA configuration also raises the commission coefficient from $0.00002$ to $0.00005$, even though it lowers the half-spread coefficient from $0.0001$ to $0.00002$. Because a portfolio-level policy can rebalance up to its full weight across all eleven assets on a single day, whereas the SAA's own trades are capped by the action-limiting factor $\kappa_t$ (Section~\ref{sec:saa}), the PAA commits comparatively larger notional per name per rebalance; its evaluation in Section~\ref{sec:paa-results} is accordingly expected, and configured, to realize a materially higher, and more realistic, effective cost per unit of turnover than reported here. This was a deliberate choice of design, as the main goal of the SAA was to find timing signals for each asset.

Because the SAA's only defensive lever is retreating into cash, it being long-only (Section~\ref{sec:saa}), and because both Asset $\alpha$ and Market $\alpha$ are scored against a fully-invested buy-and-hold counterfactual that never earns this carry (Section~\ref{sec:excess-return}), the same defensive decision is cushioned by a materially different risk-free return depending on which block it happens to fall in. Figure~\ref{fig:saa-agg-val00}'s 100\% Cash (EFFR) curve makes this concrete: it alone accounts for $+4.1\%$ of val\_00's episode return, more than the $+1.7\%$ earned by the same defensive lever in Figure~\ref{fig:saa-agg-val02}'s val\_02. The SAA's own observation does include a $60$-day $z$-score of this rate and the sub-portfolio's excess return over it ($z_t^{\text{rf}}$, $\alpha_t^{\text{rf}}$, Section~\ref{sec:saa}), so the policy itself is not blind to the prevailing regime; but Table~\ref{tab:saa-per-asset-metrics}'s summary statistics cannot separate how much of a given block's result reflects this carry from how much reflects genuine price-timing skill. Some part of the higher period-average Sharpe and Calmar reported for val\_00 (Sharpe $1.65$, Calmar $2.33$) relative to val\_01 (Sharpe $0.98$, Calmar $1.33$) may therefore reflect the level of the risk-free rate the agent was earning while defensively positioned, not only a difference in how well it timed its entries and exits.

These properties matter directly for Section~\ref{sec:info-flow}'s shadow-portfolio mechanism, since it is precisely this policy, unchanged, that is copied once per asset and queried every step to produce $a_t^{(i)}$ and $\rho_t^{(i)}$ for the PAA's asset tokens (Section~\ref{sec:paa-state}). A signal that is high-frequency, confined to a single instrument's own position, long-only, and only intermittently right about its own asset is not a ready-made allocation weight; it is one further, noisy input the PAA's attention mechanism (Section~\ref{sec:paa-network}) must learn to weigh against the raw market features, the asset's own market-relative and cross-sectional statistics, and the other ten assets' own signals. Gold's val\_03 row makes the risk of over-crediting this signal concrete: agent return $57.68\%$, Sharpe $3.89$, Sortino $5.80$, and Calmar $14.71$ are the strongest figures anywhere in the table, yet Asset $\alpha$ is only $0.58\%$. The agent essentially held Gold through an exceptional, low-volatility rally (buy-and-hold return $57.10\%$); the risk-adjusted statistics mostly restate that trend rather than demonstrate timing skill. Nothing in $a_t^{(i)}$ or $\rho_t^{(i)}$ alone distinguishes this case from a genuinely skillful one, a residual risk the PAA inherits rather than resolves on its own.

Val\_02 taken as a whole (Figure~\ref{fig:saa-agg-val02}) shows the checkpoint neither beating its own assets' passive baselines nor the broader market, during exactly the kind of correlated shock Section~\ref{sec:saa}'s single-asset design is least equipped to handle. Crude's $-37.85\%$ maximum drawdown in the same block shows that a favorable terminal alpha figure can still conceal a severe intra-episode drawdown, which is why the drawdown-aware reward terms of Section~\ref{sec:drawdown} and the Calmar ratio reported alongside raw returns are necessary complements to reading Table~\ref{tab:saa-per-asset-metrics} by final alpha alone.

\subsubsection{Implementation Plan for the Three New Agents}
\label{sec:paa-implementation}

The \textbf{control ablation} never loads or runs the frozen SAA at all, at training time or at inference time, which makes it computationally cheaper than the hierarchical system rather than merely architecturally identical to it. In place of the SAA's own forward pass, the signal injected into each asset's token is drawn from an autoregressive, order-one (AR(1)) noise process, clipped to the same range as the SAA's own action output. An AR(1) process is chosen deliberately over independent, identically distributed (i.i.d.) noise: because it is autocorrelated from one day to the next, it is at least superficially smooth in the way a genuine trading signal is smooth, which is a harder, and therefore more informative, control condition than noise a network could trivially learn to ignore as pure static. Crucially, this noise is still committed to each asset's real, isolated shadow sub-portfolio through the same shadow-portfolio bookkeeping already described in Section~\ref{sec:info-flow}, so the shadow holding percentage and the other shadow-derived diagnostics injected alongside the signal remain populated exactly as they are for the hierarchical system; only the origin of the committed action changes, from a frozen policy to a stochastic process.

The \textbf{cross-sectional-only ablation} is implemented as the zero-valued special case of the same mechanism: the injected signal is held at a constant zero rather than drawn from the AR(1) process, and the corresponding shadow sub-portfolio is simply never traded. This is a deliberate design choice rather than an implementation shortcut. Implementing this ablation as a genuinely smaller network, one whose asset token omits the SAA-signal dimension entirely, would confound two different explanations for any performance gap: a loss of information, and a loss of model capacity. Holding the signal at zero instead keeps this ablation, the control ablation, and the hierarchical system at identical architecture and identical parameter count, differing only in what occupies two input columns of the asset token. This isolates the information content of the SAA's signal specifically, which is the comparison Section~\ref{sec:paa-comparisons} actually needs.

The \textbf{monolithic end-to-end baseline} is the only one of the three that must be built substantially from new code, since neither the shadow-signal injection mechanism nor the split asset/portfolio token design of Section~\ref{sec:paa-state} presuppose a frozen, per-asset temporal specialist to consume. The simulator itself, its reward, its friction model, and its purged evaluation protocol are reused entirely unchanged, as is the surrounding validation and checkpointing infrastructure, since none of that infrastructure depends on what kind of network is being trained. What is genuinely new is a single policy that supplies its own temporal memory over raw, per-asset market features and performs both timing and cross-sectional allocation jointly, without ever being told which sub-decision is which. Wherever possible, this baseline is built to reuse the hierarchical system's own architectural building blocks, for instance replacing the frozen SAA's contribution with a trainable, per-asset recurrent memory feeding directly into the same shared-attention-plus-private-heads structure described in Section~\ref{sec:paa-network}, rather than adopting an unrelated architecture from the literature. The reasoning behind this choice is methodological: if the monolithic baseline is built from wholly different architectural primitives, any performance gap between it and the hierarchical system could be attributed to that unrelated difference rather than to the modular-versus-monolithic distinction this thesis is actually testing. Training compute, measured either in total environment steps or in wall-clock time, is matched explicitly against the budget spent training the hierarchical system, so that the comparison in Section~\ref{sec:paa-comparisons} is not confounded by one system simply being trained longer than the other. Because this baseline is the most implementation-intensive of the three, it is explicitly time-boxed: if training time does not permit it before submission, its omission is reported as a limitation of this thesis (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}) rather than silently left out of this chapter.

\subsubsection{Statistical Robustness}
\label{sec:paa-robustness}

Once the comparisons of Table~\ref{tab:paa-comparisons} exist, they are subjected to the same treatment already applied to the SAA in Section~\ref{sec:saa-robustness}: a peak-alpha checkpoint is identified on the four validation blocks, a validation-decay curve is reported around that peak as evidence for or against overfitting, and both are cross-referenced against PAA-specific training diagnostics analogous to those described in Section~\ref{sec:saa-diagnostics}. Two diagnostics are specific to the PAA's own architecture and have no SAA equivalent: the stability of the attention weights the shared \texttt{TransformerEncoder} (Section~\ref{sec:paa-network}) assigns to each asset token across checkpoints, and the degree of divergence between the actor and critic losses described in Section~\ref{sec:actor-critic-theory}, which the private per-head encoder layers of Section~\ref{sec:paa-network} are specifically designed to absorb.
